# 10: Now You Can Have the Relationship You’ve Always Wanted

In _[Social Studies]_, Fran Lebowitz made a list of things that shouldn't concern children. Making contractual obligations was on the list. Lindsay Gibson argues that keeping up the [enmeshment] with emotionally immature parents into one's adulthood is akin to continuing to live by the rules of a contract signed in one's childhood.

> You unwittingly “signed” a relationship contract with them in childhood without realizing what it would cost you in your adult life. Thankfully, you can now revise that old relationship arrangement to be fairer to you.

If you entered a contractual obligation as a child, it is your responsibility to renegotiate such a contract in adulthood. This is what it means to grow up. If you finally broke the old patterns with your parents, the ones where they expect you to be a [passive audience] to their [tantrums], congratulations. You have voided the unfair contract.

> Your mission was accomplished as soon as you asked to talk. That act alone reversed your old childhood role. By sharing your concern, you changed your old relationship contract (such as “I agree not to speak my mind around you”). Short talks like this show both of you that your connection can survive some honesty and leave things feeling more real.

Two adults are always [co-equals], no matter their relationship or the terms of the contracts one of them was manipulated into signing in their tender age. By breaking the terms of such an unfair contract, you have shown that it is now void.

> You acted like an equal and took the lead with clear, intimate communication. This is a huge step forward.

To break the old patterns, stop being susceptible to the [emotional contagion] of an emotionally immature person (EIP) in your life. When such a person tries to stir up negativity, kill it with kindness. Negativity has [nowhere to go] when the tone of the conversation shifts into a more positive direction.

> Respond to the EIP’s hostility with empathy as if they were looking for understanding rather than a fight. By discerning their deeper emotional desire for connection, you interpret their unpleasant behavior as a cry for attention and acceptance. Sometimes the surprise of an empathetic response transforms a belligerent situation and instead allows something creative, meaningful, and connecting to take place.

Toddlers, or adults who behave like toddlers, need to be able to [cry it out]. Let them process their feelings in solitude. You don't have to be there and take it. They will get better if you leave them alone. One way to help them deal with their emotions is to offer a reward they could reach for as they pull themselves out of the tantrum.

> I can see you’re really sad, Mom. I’m going to let you work it out. When you’re ready, I’ll be downstairs, and we can go shopping like we planned. But I want you to take as much time as you need to feel sad.

Don't try to change anyone's mind about anything, especially when you're talking to an emotionally immature person. Let them see you having fun without them. Maybe they'll join you. Letting people figure things out on their own is the best persuasion tactic.

> Please don’t do anything you don’t want to. We’re going to leave in about a half hour. If you change your mind, we’d love to have you go with us, or you can catch a cab over later if you’d like to join us for coffee and dessert.

Redirecting your attention from participating in an unpleasant interaction to merely observing it could save you from being emotionally coerced. If you're starting to feel manipulated, mentally leave the interaction. Pretend you're conducting an [anthropological study].

> To avoid emotional takeovers, pay attention when an EIP puts pressure on you to take responsibility for validating or fixing their feelings. When you experience that tug to make them feel better at your own expense, you can interrupt the takeover by staying aware of what they’re doing. It also helps to narrate their behavior with self-talk like the following: Now they’re trying to emotionally coerce me and make me feel bad. Now they’re inviting me to spin up into their drama triangle. Now they’re on the “me” channel. Every topic goes back to them. Now they’re dismissing and disrespecting my inner experience. Now they’re questioning my right to have my own feelings and thoughts. Now they’re challenging my duty to take care of myself first. Now they’re making me feel guilty so they can seem blameless.

To illustrate the power of the mind to get one through unpleasant interactions with difficult people, Lindsay Gibson highlights a success story her patient has shared with her. This person used to struggle when facing her mother, but once she reframed her thinking, the interactions became fulfilling to her.

> She interacted pleasantly with her mother as just another interaction with an elderly person.

The daughter was able to turn unpleasant interactions around without forcing any behavioral changes on her mother. Instead, the daughter changed the way she thought of her mother. She no longer held an expectation of emotional intimacy that one would normally reserve for family members. Sometimes spending time with strangers gives us something that we wish we could get from our family members. That might be simply because we place lower expectations on strangers. So, the trick is to lower your expectations for your family members. If you make them seem like strangers to you, then you may discover that you prefer relating to them on that level. Make each interaction a self-contained event; pretend you have no emotional baggage with this person. This is what ultimately made Gibson's patient content with the interactions she had with her difficult mother.

> She accepted each little interaction with her mother on its own terms without comparing her mother’s behavior to what she used to wish for. Once she decided to treat each interaction as a new moment, without grudges or hope, feelings of bitterness faded. She felt satisfied with whatever interactions they had.
