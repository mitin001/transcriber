# 9: Updating Your Self-Concept

Allowing yourself to learn a technical skill is one way you can show yourself that you are important, even if your parents were too emotionally immature to make you believe that in your childhood. When you're the only person in the room who can solve an important problem for your business or your patient, that certainly convinces you of your importance. So, not only does learning new skills broaden your [inner world], but it can also heal the emotional trauma from your childhood. Helpful as it is, broadening your inner world is not the only thing you can do to learn how you truly feel about yourself. You should also understand and disregard feelings of shame.

> See shame as a helpful warning that someone is trying to make you feel bad so they can feel better about themselves. Seeing shame for what it is not only helps free you from others’ emotional coercions but repairs your **self-concept** as well.

[Guilt and shame] are temporary feelings meant to signal to you that you better correct your behavior or re-evaluate your relationships. Both of these emotions should be fleeting, not lasting. You can let go of guilt and shame by exploring what caused them and rethinking how you should continue to interact with the people who foisted those unpleasant emotions onto you (and whether you should at all).

> Repeatedly exploring childhood shame feelings and then relabeling them correctly—as highly unpleasant emotions, not the truth of you—shrinks shame back into a manageable emotion, not a declaration of your worth as a human being. By questioning shame, you can unmask it as just an emotion foisted on you by others, instead of being your core identity. Instead of absorbing shame into your self-concept, you could treat it as just another emotion.

Shame is a tool that an emotionally immature person (EIP) uses against psychologically defenseless people. Once you understand this, you can replace shame with empathy. You should feel empathy for yourself, not shame. "I'm so ashamed" should become "they had no right to shame me, but they did it anyway, so I need to reduce and [carefully manage] interactions with them."

> The only truth about shame is that an EIP probably made you feel awful about yourself at an age when you were psychologically defenseless. As an adult, you can defuse shame feelings by exposing the erroneous self-belief that underlies it, looking at it without running away, and then questioning it.

Emotionally immature people use dirty tricks like shame in their emotional coercions because they lack the capacity to achieve a true emotional understanding of themselves or others. These are the same people who regard [empty, vapid pronouncements] as wisdom and reduce human beings to [props] on their stage. They can't grasp individual differences and the rich complexity of each person's inner world, so they classify people into stereotypes. They assign a person a personality type (e.g., INFP) or a zodiac sign (e.g., Scorpio) and rely on it to justify to everyone that they know the person better than the person knows themself.

> They lump people together, seeing them as much more similar than they really are. Individual complexities are ignored as they say things like, “You’re just like your father!” or “You’re like my side of the family.” They think they know you because you remind them of someone. As a result, they give you a typecast self-concept that doesn’t really fit you. With EI parents, you were told what to be, not helped to discover who you are. But now as an adult, you can expand your self-concept to include all your potential and complexity, even if EIPs still pigeonhole you in an oversimplified or childlike identity. You don’t have to go through life feeling like less than you are. Thankfully, your self-concept no longer has to be held hostage by your parents’ opinions. You are now free to discover who you are and what you want to become. You can update your self-concept to fit who you really are.
