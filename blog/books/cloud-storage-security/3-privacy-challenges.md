# 3. Privacy Challenges

> If a tree falls in the forest and no one hears it, does it make a sound? If someone collects your personal information and it does not harm you, does it matter? Many people consider cloud data security and privacy concerns unnecessary because they believe (1) their data has value only to them, (2) they have herd immunity giving them a low risk, or (3) they have some security protection so attackers will focus on more vulnerable targets.

Everyone overestimates their luck. Everyone believes that trouble won't ever find them. Everyone thinks that identity theft and the like happen to other people, someone more famous or more vulnerable. Why would someone steal the private data of an average civilian? The answer is that technology has made everything scalable, even crime.

> More sophisticated tools and computing resources mean more individuals and smaller organizations become low hanging fruit.

Everyone's data has value to criminals now.

> To get an estimate of the value of your data and the ease of obtaining it, we can look at the prices for stolen data and hacking services.

Criminals commit crimes under stolen identities. They need a steady supply of those to keep doing their jobs.

> The researchers found an uptick in counterfeit documents in 2014. Data collected from various online sites make it easier to obtain or forge such documents. Prices for doxing services (including data collection from social media, public records, social engineering, and installing malware) range from US$25 to US$100. User names and passwords for verifiable online bank accounts cost about 6% of the account value. The price to have a website hacked can cost from US$100 to US$200. **Fullz**, a complete set of credentials to commit identify theft, typically including full name, address, phone number, email, Social Security Number, bank account and credit card information, sells for US$35-45.

Fullz is cheaper than doxing. Doxing is cheaper than hacking. Hacking is cheaper than kitz, especially if they contain health insurance data.

> The medical and health insurance data can significantly increase the prices for fullz and **kitz**, which provide everything needed for a complete fake identity. Kitz with verifiable health insurance range from US$1200 to US$1500 each. Fullz with US health insurance sell for around US$500. Health insurance credentials alone (including names, dates of birth, plan information, and insurer contact information for filing claims) have a value of about $20, plus an equal amount for each additional plan like dental or vision.

Medical identity theft is particularly lucrative and particularly brazen.

> Victims of medical identity theft more often knew the thief.

So, if you're investigating a case of medical identity theft, look through the victim's contacts. If there are no leads there, look at datasets with patient health data that have found their way online. Healthcare workers can be quite careless with patient data.

> Residents at a medical university in 2013 used Google Drive and Google Mail for collaborative sharing of patient data. Patient data in the files included patient names, addresses, ages, medical record numbers, dates, provider name, diagnosis, and prognosis.

Software companies can be just as careless with the private data of their users. If you downloaded your data from Facebook in 2013, the export included information from the address books of your Facebook friends. In those days, to get the email and phone number of a high-profile individual, all you had to do was befriend someone connected to them on Facebook.

> A bug in Facebook’s “Download Information” in 2013 exposed users email and telephone numbers.

Greedy insurance companies keep setting your premiums higher and higher. They come to [data brokers] to buy your personal data to find dirt on you and list it as a justification for those higher premiums.

> Insurance companies collect demographic and lifestyle data to build risk profiles of individuals and set their insurance premiums accordingly. Insurance companies can mine public cloud data for information to support not paying a claim or to increase a premium. While understandable from the insurance company perspective, the cloud data owner still has their data used to their detriment and not for the purpose for which they made it available.

If a criminal stole your data and leaked it online, insurance companies will find it and use it as a reason to deny your claims. The line between oversharing and data theft is murky, and this is something that can be exploited by these companies for profit.

> Employers have an interest in cloud data of their employees. This data can inform the employer about things employees do or think that could affect the company’s reputation, the employee’s job performance, or company costs in health insurance. For example, many companies want to charge their employees an extra fee for the higher costs of health insurance.

Do not post anything that could put you at a disadvantage at work. In other words, do not post anything.

> Even a photo of a rare smoking incident, such as a cigar at a special event, could cast doubt on the employees healthy life-style habits.

Do not allow your employer or insurance company to put trackers on you.

> Companies have begun offering employees incentives to promote healthy lifestyles, such as fitness tracking devices and programs. These devices collect health data and store it in the cloud.

Opt out of this if you can. We do not live in a surveillance state. This is one of the privileges of living in a free country. Do not volunteer to surrender this privilege.

> While voluntary, it does mean employees remain under surveillance by their employers. Furthermore, the devices have little or minimal security. Employers could track employee movement in and out of work, or even check heart rates during meetings to know who feels stressed.

Expect them to find ways to use the data collected on you to your disadvantage.

> Employers often give psychological tests to prospective employees.

But they don't have to anymore, not in the world of Facebook, where your likes can be used to build your psychological profile with astounding accuracy. And if the accuracy isn't so astounding, they won't care. If your likes happen to mistakenly classify you as having undesirable personality traits, you will suffer the unintended consequences.

> The Philip Dick novel “Minority Report” describes a world where the government pre-convicted people for future crimes based on predictions of possible future behavior. The intent to prevent crime could be used to justify access to all cloud storage data to look for patterns typically seen in those who do commit crimes. When marketers make a profiling error, you get an irrelevant advertisement. When government agencies make profiling errors, it can cost a person a great deal in time and money to fix the mistake. Consider the cases of people put on no-fly lists for no apparent reason and the difficulties they have in correcting the error.

The laws to protect our privacy lag behind the technology that can violate it.

> Privacy laws regarding digital media remain ambiguous and inconsistent, so we should expect difficulties because of misinterpretations.

Laws aren't like flaws in tech. They can't be tested without affecting real people. Whereas most of the flaws in tech products are exposed and fixed before their release, the laws can only be tested in the real world. The time it takes law enforcement to understand the law is the time in which wrongful convictions will proliferate.

> A person’s Fourth Amendment right does not protect them from search and seizure when law enforcement officials make genuine mistakes about the interpretation of the law.

This is especially consequential in war zones.

> Israel security officials routinely demand access to email of those crossing between Israel and the Palestinian Territories. Those that refuse are expelled as terrorists.

A country will allow itself to commit privacy violations, citing an ongoing war as justification. Searches and seizures of personal devices were once unthinkable in Russia but became routine once this country invaded its neighbor, Ukraine. What's even more alarming is that a search and seizure of data across country lines can occur even in times of peace.

> A court in the United States ordered Microsoft to provide emails from a customer for a drug investigation. However, the emails existed on Microsoft servers in Dublin, Ireland. Microsoft refused citing privacy and legal jurisdiction of the search warrant. However, a US court ruled that search warrants for online data actual represent document subpoenas, and determined that Microsoft had to provide the email because they could take the email from the servers in Ireland.

When one jurisdiction has laws that are supposed to protect its citizens from data seizures but another jurisdiction criminalizes noncompliance with its data seizure requests, how do we resolve this deadlock?

> Regardless of which country you store your online data, if you can get to it then the US government can demand it from you. However, this case shows the possibility that those who store others’ data in the cloud could find themselves in a situation where complying with a law in one country results in breaking a law in another country.

If the geographical distribution of data centers occasionally leads cloud providers to stumble into jurisdictional collisions, the situation with P2P systems can be downright dire. [P2P] can be a massive liability because it is easy to misconfigure. Malicious entities routinely scan the P2P network for private files inadvertently left accessible by it.

> A Pentagon contractor with top-security security clearance copied sensitive Department of Defense documents to a personal computer that had P2P software installed, resulting in these documents getting leaked.

What you post online can get people jailed or killed.

> Oppressive governments will scan data stored in the cloud for evidence of political dissent. These governments will collect whatever data they can and weave fragments of it together to support their accusations of those who challenge them.

What you post on social media in the US can be used against your friends from other countries, especially those with authoritarian governments. Remember the Green Movement protests in Iran in 2009.

> The Iranian government arrested or harassed its citizens who had relatives in other countries who criticized the government on social media.

If you need to publicize your private communications, it is worth it to take the time to go through them beforehand to redact the parts that belong to others. We would all do well to learn from Jeb Bush's mistake.

> Jeb Bush recently released hundreds of thousands of his emails from his term as Florida governor. These emails contained sensitive information about other people, including their email addresses, home addresses, telephone numbers, Social Security Numbers, and medical information.

Of course, you should redact parts of what you're posting that belong to you, too, if you'd rather not let it be used to harm you.

> Some people have become victims of fraud because they thoughtlessly posted selfies while holding their first paycheck. Criminals found the images by searching Instagram photos for specific hashtags, then read the account and routing numbers from the images.

Social media can get you hacked in subtler ways, too. It holds a treasure trove of information to bypass knowledge-based authentication.

> The attack on the Apple iCloud accounts of celebrities in 2014 happened because attackers guessed things like user names, passwords, and answers to security questions.

Do not post embarrassing photos of your children. Leave this particular activity to the analog world. Once you're in the digital world, you can no longer guarantee that you know everyone that can access the photos you've shared.

> Before the Internet, many children and adults experienced the trauma of having their parents show embarrassing photos to friends who came to visit. Internet social media has the potential to amplify this trauma by exposing the photos to more people and making them harder to forget.

Do not post any details about your children that could harm them.

> Some parents use social media to discuss medical, psychological, or disciplinary issues with their children.

You don't know everyone who can access this information, and some of the people you don't know may intend to harm your child. You don't know how the information in their possession will be used in the future. Expect it to remain accessible permanently. Consider how it may harm the child once they grow up.

> Social media includes vastly more people in the discussion and effectively leaves a permanent record.

The Internet can both outlast and ruin a person. Do not give it any more ammunition than it already has.
