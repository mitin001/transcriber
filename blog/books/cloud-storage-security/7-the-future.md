# 7. The Future

There's a difference between storing data and storing information. Data can be anything. Information is structured data. Eliyahu Goldratt defines information as data that is structured in the question-answer format.

> Information is the answer to the question asked.

Storage devices can store data no matter how it's structured. If you can find a material that can be divided into an astronomical number of addressable locations, where the state of each location can be durably modified without affecting the others, then you can invent a storage device. Magnetic material on the cylinders of a hard disk is one such material: magnetizing an addressable location on it changes the bit value at that location. Transistors within solid-state drives are another such material.

> Hard drives were first invented by IBM in the 1950s and were commercially available in 1956. While the basic technology using magnetic media has been improved and miniaturized, the basic technological concept has remained the same: a spinning disk (or cylinder) coated with a ferromagnetic material that is magnetized in one of two directions, which is used to represent a binary “1” or “0.” The 1’s and 0’s are written and read at specific addressable locations on the disk to provide the ability to store data. Solid state drives on the other hand, use semiconductor chips instead of magnetic media to store data. There are no moving mechanical parts. Binary 1’s and 0’s are represented using transistors that are switched either on or off. The advantage is that solid state drives have faster access times. Their disadvantage is that they tend to decay over time because of the underlying physics of the transistor and connections tend to build up electrical resistance with repeated erase/write cycles. Currently available solid state devices can deliver up to 50,000 erase/write cycles before this occurs.

Clearly, HDDs and SSDs leave a lot of room for improvement in the durability of storage technology. Research is underway to repurpose the nuclei of phosphorus atoms as storage material. Tilting the spin of such a nucleus could constitute a write operation. Researchers are currently looking for ways to keep this system stable at temperatures above absolute zero.

> At Canada’s Simon Fraser University (SFU) researchers have encoded information in the form of quantum bits, called qubits, into the nuclei of phosphorus atoms held in a purified substrate of silicon. They used magnetic field pulses to tilt the spin of the nuclei to “write” qubit memory. Since qubits rely on the spin of nuclei, it is important that these materials are kept near absolute zero, which minimizes noise at the atomic level and increases the stability of qubits. What makes the SFU research interesting is that they were able to maintain qubit stability at room temperature for 39 minutes and were able to manipulate qubits as the temperature climbed and fell back toward absolute zero.

Not only could quantum computing improve the durability of data storage, but it also promises to [solve] the problems that are computationally intractable on a classical computer.

> In a quantum system, a qubit is stored in a superposition state where it can represent both 1 and 0 at the same time. This property, as researchers note, enables qubits to perform multiple calculations simultaneously.

[Quantum entanglement] (i.e., the existence of entangled quantum states) models an exponential mathematical structure (tensor products), and it's conceivable that certain exponential-complexity problems can be mapped onto that system and solved efficiently by quantum computers.

> If a set of entangled qubits are in different locations, a change in value of a qubit at one location will also be reflected in the qubit value in another location without any physical connection.

Because it's impossible to read qubits without altering them, it is harder to [eavesdrop on quantum communications]. Consequently, the future promises to be much more secure than the present when it comes to data storage. And yet, Apple and Google decided not to wait for quantum computers and started improving the security of classical computers. Their devices already encrypt the data stored on them by default.

> This encryption applies to data on the device itself and not to data backed up to cloud storage. These providers still have the ability to give the unencrypted data in response to law enforcement requests.

Baby steps. The backups of data on encrypted devices are stored in the cloud either unencrypted or encrypted with a key shared with the cloud providers. With smartphones, everyone has become a citizen sensor. We have effectively become cloud-tethered devices that spy on themselves and everyone around them. Putting smartphone-like capabilities on eyeglasses (e.g., Google Glass) would turn the world into one large surveillance state.

> Such devices transform people into data collection tools. Every person with such a device effectively becomes a mobile CCTV camera recording the activities of every person in their view, in addition to recording their own activities and what attracts their attention. Fast facial recognition algorithms and dedicated processors will advance us toward real-time identification and tracking of everyone, connected or not.

Such tools can be abused with devastating repercussions.

> The Frank family in 1942 Nazi Germany would not have gotten to a safe house unobserved.

If historical analogies don't scare you, there's plenty to be scared of in the present. For example, how sure can you be that the Internet is not watching your baby monitor?

> Data watchdog groups found a Russian-based website showing live feeds of webcams from baby monitors.

It can go even deeper. When cybercriminals can see not just into your home but into your essence as a human being, the promise of a high-tech utopia can quickly turn into an Orwellian dystopia.

> Using DNA, we may become the storage device where all of us, together, become the cloud.

So, as always, it's a race between technological progress and innovations in security that would keep it from turning the world into a tech hellscape. There is a glimmer of hope for ultimate security for our private data, but it might not arrive on time, before our most private data gets into the wrong hands.
