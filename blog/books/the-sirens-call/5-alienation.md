# 5. Alienation

Artisanship is a deeply satisfying experience. This is how people are meant to work.

> He gets to oversee an arc of progression from raw materials to finished product. In the end, he has produced an object that he has authorship of. It is his. And then he can sell it, taking money in exchange for the good.

Unfortunately, it is going extinct as the assembly line of capitalism supplants it. An artisan is replaced with an assembly line worker.

> He mans a machine that does the same thing—stamping an insole, for instance—over and over, hour after hour, day after day, week after week. What was once the craftsman’s own—his technique and ability, his object—is now the capitalist’s. The laborer is a mere means to an end.

Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels wrote in the Communist Manifesto about us becoming mere cogs in the machines of the aristocrats.

> The work of the proletarians has lost all individual character, and, consequently, all charm for the workman. He becomes an appendage of the machine, and it is only the most simple, most monotonous, and most easily acquired knack, that is required of him.

The aristocrat owns the finished product. It was produced by laborers on _his_ assembly line. Neither the laborers nor the aristocrats are attached to the product in the same way an artisan would be.

> Each shoe made by our cobbler is distinct to him, whereas each hour of labor on the assembly line stamping soles onto shoes can be done by anyone. The result of all of this at a subjective psychological level is, Marx says, alienation. The craftsman has ownership of the object he produces through his labor, even as he then sells it in a market exchange. His effort and skill and toil have been poured into the object, and at the end of the process he is the one who owns the object. He then chooses to sell it as part of a market exchange, and in so doing transfers the ownership of the finished product to the customer in exchange for money. The assembly line worker, by contrast, may never even see the finished shoes. He may never own a pair himself. And he has little to no pride of authorship in them.

This produces a culture of alienation where people are dehumanized and their identities are lost. Office work of the present is no different. We stare all day at screens only to come back home to stare at screens.

> There’s a picture of a man in an office, dressed in standard business casual attire, forlornly working at a desktop computer with the caption: “Tired of looking at BAD SCREEN.” Beneath this image, there’s a picture of the same man in a T-shirt, sitting in bed, happily typing on his laptop: “Can’t wait to get home and look at GOOD SCREEN.” It turns out the stultifying attentional atmosphere of the modern workplace gives rise to a craving for relief satiated by the same devices out of which the prison of boredom is itself constructed.

Not only is our labor now extracted from us for profit, but also our attention.

> Attention is now commodified and can be traded, bought, and sold in sophisticated, instantaneous algorithmic auctions that price a second of our eyes’ focus.

Just as becoming a cog in someone else's machine is a dehumanizing experience, becoming a number in someone's algorithm is a way to lose your individuality completely.

> Human work has always existed, but wage labor is a creation of industrial capitalism. Human attention has always existed, but “clicks,” “content,” “engagement,” and “eyeballs” are creations of attention capitalism. And to be reduced to a wage or an eyeball is to find oneself alienated from some part of oneself.

Now that we no longer talk to each other in any way other than through the algorithm, we are no longer the deciders of where and to whom we direct our attention, a complete loss of individuality. Meanwhile, the people who are extracting our attention from us to enrich themselves don't even know what they're doing.

> The commodification of attention has gotten so widespread and the markets that buy and sell that attention have, through increasingly complex and opaque computerized transactions, become divorced from the actual attention of real human beings that is supposedly being bought and sold. This has, like the housing market, opened tremendous opportunities for gaming the system. Quite notoriously, Meta, which along with Google is one half of the ad-tech duopoly that captures roughly half of all internet advertising revenue, has been caught repeatedly wildly overstating its own attention measurement metrics to advertisers.

The aristocrats of today are hopelessly incompetent. These are the same types of morons and crooks that have caused the financial crisis of 2008.

> The problem in the great financial crisis was the enormous growth of “subprime” loans that were sliced and diced into securities that banks and investors treated as rock-solid when they were not. The commodification and engineering grew so abstract that markets no longer knew what they were trading. They constructed entire edifices atop a substance they thought was solid rock but turned out to be quicksand.

And now we're barreling towards the economic crisis of what Tim Hwang calls subprime attention.

> Half my advertising spend is wasted; the trouble is, I don’t know which half.

Investment of capital goes toward attention, not the human connected to it. At least half the time, the ad dollars are wasted. The other half is made largely of nonsense under the gotcha banner "made you look".

> In 1835, The Sun, voracious for new readers to support its advertising sales, published a bombshell six-story cover series about life on the moon. Claimed to be drawn from a Scottish science journal about a powerful telescope in South Africa that could view life on the moon, the detailed accounts of teeming wildlife—unicorns and beavers, presided over by a race of half-men, half-bat aliens—became a sensation. In the dreary days of August when it was published, The Sun’s circulation exploded. Given the competitive attention market, other newspapers responded with their own articles about The Sun’s reporting, either seeking to repackage or rebut it, but all attempting to leverage the attention to the story for their own profit. The contemporary version of this is the social media post that claims to have some mind-blowing secret knowledge and goes viral, only for someone to chime in to say, “Well, actually this just isn’t true.” Maybe the original poster deletes it. Maybe they don’t. Either way, it has achieved its aim, which was to make you look.

We are susceptible to rage-baiting. Attention-hungry politicians yank our chains. We waste our time arguing over nonsense as the world heads toward the climate catastrophe. The data centers powering the algorithm that presides over our lives are slowly suffocating us with carbon dioxide.
