# 1. The Sirens’ Call

We often don't get to choose where we direct our attention.

> If someone fires a gun in the air, your attention will instantly shift to the sound even before you can fully grasp what’s happening. Attention can be extracted from us at the purely sensory level, before our conscious will even gets to weigh in. In fact, this is how a siren functions.

Attention-grabbing is universal. Everyone is susceptible to it, and every community takes advantage of it. We instantly recognize a siren in any city on Earth as an emergency no matter what it sounds like.

> No matter where you are, its call is at once familiar and foreign. The foreignness comes from the fact that in different countries the siren sounds slightly different—elongated, or two-toned, or distinctly pitched. But even if you’ve never encountered it before, you instantly understand its purpose. Amidst a language you may not speak and food you’ve never tried, the siren is universal. It exists to grab our attention, and it succeeds.

Social commentators of the day commonly condemn the attention-grabbing nature of the phone as being so detrimental to our society they liken it to cigarettes. Breathless protests of our elders against video games or television have taught us have shown to us that sometimes we are warned in vain. The extreme case of such an exercise in Luddite thinking is the protestations Socrates voiced against adopting the technology of the written word. Humans must rely on their memory, he argued. Anything that helps it cheapens the experience, apparently.

> In Plato’s Phaedrus, Socrates goes on a long rant—half persuasive and half ludicrous—about the peril posed by the new technology of…writing: “If men learn [the art of writing],” Socrates warns, “it will implant forgetfulness in their souls: They will cease to exercise memory because they rely on that which is written, calling things to remembrance no longer from within themselves, but by means of external marks. What you have discovered is a recipe not for memory, but for reminder.”

It seems as though before it gets accepted and widely adopted, every new technology goes through the same cycle of hype and smear.

> Excitement and wonder that quickly turn to dread and panic.

Meanwhile, the thing we ought to be dreading the most is easy to overlook because, as climate activist Bill McKibben points out, it is invisible.

> The most dangerous thing on the planet [CO2] is invisible, odorless, tasteless, and doesn’t actually do anything to you directly.

No, the thing we are warned the most about nowadays is not the climate catastrophe. It is information overload. We are being buried in the grey goo of information to the point where we can no longer focus on what's important.

> Information is infinite and attention is limited. And value derives from scarcity, which is why attention is so valuable.

As soon as software companies identify which parts of their product get the attention, they enshittify it with ads and just about anything else that adds to its revenue stream. The top of the Google search results page used to give us what we were looking for. Now it's all ads and various widgets designed to prevent us from clicking away. Amazon went even further with its search results page.

> In many cases, Amazon has seen which products dominate that attentional space and then started producing them itself, cutting out the middleman.

Amazon gathered the data on what people are searching on its website and used it to create offbrands of those thereby enshittifying not only what's on the screen but the physical goods people were buying.

> To call Amazon a “retailer” misstates the source of its market power. Amazon is an attention and logistics company, and the products it sells are an afterthought.

In the attention age, we can't have nice things.

> In the attention age, even the sale of stuff to consumers has more to do with getting their attention than making the stuff itself.

This is the consequence of the shifting of priorities from product to brand, that is, from manufacturing to marketing. 

> The relationship between product and brand had grown more and more attenuated. The brand now stood as the dominant feature of the product: not the shoe itself but the little swoosh stitched onto it.

Nowadays, corporations are busy producing logos and other marketing materials. They leave the stuff that we actually want and need up to the cheapest labor that can make it. They will buy the product from the cheapest source for peanuts, stick their logo onto it, and then sell it to the consumer at a significant price markup. The more the consumer is willing to pay, the higher the price. Corporations segment their consumer base into price classes and come up with brands that appeal to each class even as the product underneath the brand sticker with the marked-up price is the same. Barry Lynn found evidence for this when he watched a recall that affected brands from multiple price classes.

> The recall revealed that high-end, expensive brands like Iams and Hill’s Pet Nutrition Science Diet rolled off the exact same Menu Foods packing lines as the cans that were wrapped in labels bearing such names as Supervalu and Price Chopper.

Products have become commodified and branded. So have people. We sell our work to corporations for a wage, which, like cheap products, they resell under their brands.

> What had previously been regarded as human effort was converted into a commodity with a price. People had always “worked” in one way or the other, but that work was now embedded in a complicated system that converted the work into a market good.

Karl Marx commented on the unfulfilling life such an arrangement brings to the worker.

> The worker therefore only feels himself outside his work, and in his work feels outside himself.

Chris Hayes points out the incompatibility of this arrangement with our society. And yet, corporations have found a way to force it onto our culture. Work is now its dominant force. Everything revolves around work for an average citizen.

> The transmutation of what had always been “work” or “things humans did for specific purposes” into “labor” as a category of activity with a price required an entire transformation of the structure of society and the daily experience of human life. Indeed, in order to extract labor from a person, you need to compensate them through wages or coerce them into it or use violence—such as the overseer’s whip—to force it out of them.

Capitalism is the worst economic system—except for all the others. The above indictment of corporations suggests an improvement to capitalism. If the worker feels unfulfilled as a cog in someone else's machine, perhaps we should let each worker own their work. To promote this change, stop giving your money to corporations and instead choose artisanal goods. Artisanship is the best of both worlds. It is compatible with our economic system while also keeping the artisans happy and actualized. If Amazon won't show artisanal products on its top page and instead reserve that space for their crappy offbrands, then there's a need to create an artisanal version of Amazon. Human ingenuity has so far been the solution to all of the ills of the human condition. Perhaps we can also figure our way out of man-made problems like corporate greed and maybe even climate change.
