# 3. The Root of Evil

> When we are alone with our thoughts, we naturally begin to contemplate our own death, and it’s intolerable.

Blaise Pascal agrees.

> The natural poverty of our feeble and mortal condition, so miserable that nothing can comfort us when we think of it closely.

As Pascal understands it, all irrational human activity stems from the fear of death and attempts to distract the mind from contemplating it.

> He wants to understand why men are tempted to undertake the hazards of war and conquest and all manner of dangerous and indeed sinful activity rather than simply enjoy what they have. The root of it, he proposes, is an aspect of the human condition upon which so much today depends: the restlessness of our minds, the craving for diversion.

The desire to busy one's mind is to steer it away from thinking about death, Pascal argues.

> This craving, he contends, emanates from a kind of spiritual angst about our own mortality.

Being bored is agonizing because, in boredom, one's mind contemplates its end. Yasmine Musharbash argues that this is only true for Western civilization.

> Pre-colonisation, there would not have been such a thing as boredom.

The Warlpiri of Australia didn't even have a word for boredom until the Westerners imported it onto their land with their habits.

> Boredom is an invasive mental state.

Musharbash explains that boredom arises when a rigid structure is imposed on one's time.

> Boredom is when you rub up against time. That just would not have happened before. Because of colonisation and how the day is structured—school bells, work times—time becomes a straitjacket.

If you don't want to lose your sanity in dark thoughts, stop driving yourself crazy with deadlines. Unfortunately, Western civilization had a different answer to solve this problem: even more distraction.

> Over the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, new technologies and lucrative attention markets grew up to solve the problem first of the leisure class and then everyone else in their hours of leisure: Where do I put my restless mind when I am not at work?

To keep imposing the 9-5 workday onto the citizenry, corporations gave us movies and television to keep us entertained when we were not at work. Television stimulated our attention like never before, and predictably, people started craving more intense stimulation once they got used to television. So, it has given way to the infinite scroll of short videos on social media. David Foster Wallace predicted this in his 1996 novel _Infinite Jest_.

> The book revolves around a mysterious tape, called simply The Entertainment, that is so entertaining, so engrossing, and so fully colonizes one’s attention that viewers literally watch the film until they die.

The web has given us technology that could keep you from ever reaching the limit of what it could give you in terms of entertainment. There was no longer any friction between cravings for new stimuli and the way to fulfill them. All we have to do is simply scroll. The webpage reacts to the scroll by loading more content tailored to your attentional preferences. It uses what you have paid attention to in the past to predict what you will pay attention to in the future, and it almost always gets it right.

> Wallace conjured the notion of an infinite entertainment as dystopian warning, but infinite entertainment is now what we confront: A film ends. A TV show ends. A board game, even a long one, ultimately ends. But the scroll on the phone has no finite limit. You could, theoretically, scroll TikTok until the end of your days. It is the source of infinite jest. In fact, the actual name for the technical feature that allows this experience harkens back to Wallace’s novel. It’s called the “infinite scroll.”

The infinite scroll isn't even the most insidious invention of the attention age. It's their attempt to give us a substitute for our need for community.

> Our minds crave things to pay attention to, but above all else we crave connection with other humans. The way out of the trap is to put our attention on other people. What really makes the attention age different from previous eras is that the attention merchants have figured that out, too.

The infinite scroll of social media feeds occasionally shows you updates from people you care about and provides you with a means of interacting with them. Once you've interacted with them on social media, there's no more need for you to see them in real life, so you can keep scrolling. There's no need to ever put your phone down.
