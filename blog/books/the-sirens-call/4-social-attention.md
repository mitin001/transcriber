# 4. Social Attention

The English phrase "paying attention" is more apt than many realize. Attention is indeed an investment in a relationship.

> We pay for a good with money, and we pay for our relationships with our attention.

Parents must invest nearly all of their attention in their children, especially at the start. Human babies are born more helpless than babies of other animals. We are born premature, before the head and the brain inside it grow to the size that would allow us any appreciable degree of autonomy. Having a mother that walks upright places a limit on how large the newborn's head can be.

> To be a parent, particularly the parent of an infant, is to have one’s attention always divided. This is the evolutionary bargain we have struck. To get our big heads and brains and to walk upright, our species gives birth to helpless creatures who demand attention.

However, when we become adults, we must learn to abandon our childhood instinct that equates seeking attention with survival.

> The Stoics and the Buddha both counseled against desiring those things outside oneself, because they made one suffer, and the attention of others was at the top of the list.

Epictetus directs us to question whether other people are even worthy of our attention-seeking.

> Who exactly are these people that you want to be admired by?

Hegel responds to this question by pointing out a paradox. Once you find the people willing to give you their attention, you no longer want their attention.

> Hegel tracks two individuals’ struggle for supremacy over each other, even up until death, until one is conquered to become the slave to the stronger master. The slave is now reduced to a supplicant and an object in the eyes of the master, purely a vessel for the master’s own wishes. But this is a false victory. For as Kojève recounts, the master desires recognition from the slave, but because he does not recognize the slave’s humanity, he cannot have it.

Kojève interprets the attention of the supplicant as attention devoid of recognition.

> For he can be satisfied only by recognition from one whom he recognizes as worthy of recognizing him.

People don't become famous looking for attention. They become famous looking for recognition. The tragedy is that they can never get recognition from their fans. Their attention is unwanted attention.

> The Star seeks recognition from the Fan, but the Fan is a stranger, who cannot be known by the Star. Because the Star cannot recognize the Fan, the Fan’s recognition of the Star doesn’t satisfy the core existential desire. There is no way to bridge the inherent asymmetry of the relationship, short of actual friendship and correspondence, but that, of course, cannot be undertaken at the same scale. And so the Star seeks recognition and gets, instead, attention.

Some stars cope with this realization by settling for attention, embracing it, and even becoming addicted to it to the detriment of us all.

> This is the story of Donald Trump’s life: wanting recognition, instead getting attention, and then becoming addicted to attention itself, because he can’t quite understand the difference, even though deep in his psyche there’s a howling vortex that fame can never fill.

Elon Musk is so addicted to attention that he was willing to throw away a large chunk of his excessive fortune just to get people to read his stupid posts on social media. He will never feel recognized no matter how much money he throws away for attention.

> So caught in the spiral of social attention was Musk that he was willing to give up a substantial part of his fortune. What does the world’s richest man want that he cannot have? What will he pay the biggest premium for? He can buy whatever he desires. There is no luxury past his grasp. But what he wants above all else, to a pathological degree, with an unsteady obsessiveness that’s thrown his fortune into question, is recognition.

Hell is not other people. Hell is our incessant desire to get the attention of other people. Epictetus reminds us that our job is to be content with ourselves no matter how little attention we might get.

> If you want some good, get it from yourself.

To be content, grow up and stop desiring the attention of others.

> The only true way to be content is to kill desire for those things outside oneself, particularly the attention of others.

This lesson is particularly important for the present times. We live in unprecedented times for solitude in the sense that we're the loneliest we've ever been.

> The very notion of a “bedroom,” a separate place where different family members can sleep in privacy, is at the earliest a seventeenth-century invention. Most people in most places throughout human history have shared a single space for eating, sleeping, and socializing. But as a society gets richer, it creates the conditions for far greater amounts of solitude, and with solitude comes the modern phenomenon of loneliness. There is a long, vast literature on the problem of loneliness as a function of modernity.

Seeking contentedness within ourselves is perhaps the best way to get through the modern age of solitude.
