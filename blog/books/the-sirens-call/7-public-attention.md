# 7. Public Attention

During the Lincoln-Douglas debate, Lincoln made a pronouncement about the importance of public sentiment in politics.

> Public sentiment is everything. With public sentiment, nothing can fail; without it, nothing can succeed.

Politicians misinterpret Lincoln's famous pronouncement in the same way they misinterpret everything: to suit their needs. In this case, it's to absolve them of the responsibility to shape public opinion, especially when it's inconvenient.

> Politicians love that Lincoln quote because they can use it to mean basically the opposite of what Lincoln intended: rather than expressing the view that politicians have responsibility over how they shape public sentiment, many politicians view public sentiment as an exogenous force, something that blows in like the weather that they must then deal with. This is a convenient position for a politician to take because, like Douglas’s position on the Supreme Court, it absolves them of responsibility. A politician can say: Well, you see, public sentiment isn’t with me, so what can I do, really?

Lincoln made his point about public sentiment to invalidate Douglas's cop-out. Douglas said there was nothing he could do about slavery because the public wants it, regardless of how immoral it is.

> Douglas’s position during the debates on the question of slavery was that the Compromise of 1850 and Dred Scott were both the law of the land and, well, like it or not, you just had to live in the real world where these were established truths. Lincoln’s point, however, was that laws like the Missouri Compromise were the product of public opinion and that prominent politicians like Stephen Douglas had a role in shaping that public opinion. It simply wouldn’t do to say, this is the way things are and there’s nothing we can do to change it; politicians had both the opportunity and the duty to use their communications with the public to move public opinion in the direction they thought best for the union.

Lincoln reminded Douglas that it was his duty to steer the public in the direction of morality, should it find itself veering too far from it. And yet, politicians today are overwhelmed by this responsibility and often prefer to relegate it to the realm of the infeasible.

> They can lose sight of their own power. They feel impotent and dependent on the whims of a public whose fleeting attention is difficult to capture and even more difficult to retain.

This is true of anyone who is supposed to have influence over the public sentiment.

> Everyone who works in public life in the attention age—in politics, or public relations, or organizing, or journalism, or anything having to do with what we call the news cycle—feels they are chasing attention rather than commanding it.

It seems that the only people who succeed at changing the public sentiment are changing it for the worse. With the rise of the Trump movement, hate speech and civil unrest are soaring. The MAGA Nation was given permission to abandon their attempts at tolerance of minority populations. The people aligned with this movement are effective at capturing public attention and directing it toward hate.

> They are intensely polarizing figures who seem addicted to controversy, and most importantly they are the most prominent examples of people who seem to compulsively feed off negative attention. Which is to say: they are trolls.

The Trump movement is a consequence of Internet trolling. It's easy to be horrible to people while interactions feel anonymous and like a video game. In video games, no crime is off limits because it's perpetrated in a simulation. Many Internet users seem to fail to grasp that social media sites are not video games: hurling insults at people on them lands in the hearts and minds of real people. Using the Internet is similar to playing a video game, and the cues the user receives from this setup seem to tell their mind that it's fine to regress into the socially immature state of a child.

> As soon as there were people on message boards, there were users who delighted in provocations and pranks and making a nuisance of themselves looking for a reaction. The basic reality of detaching mass communication and socialization from face-to-face communication means that normal inhibitors for all kinds of antisocial behavior are eradicated. People can’t see you, they don’t know who you are, and you can say things in online venues you’d never have the nerve or even impulse to say to people in person. Everyone you encounter is a disembodied little image or username, not fully human, and this form of social interaction makes the true substance of human connection—recognition, love—nearly impossible and replaces it with the chemically similar but wildly less rewarding experience of attention. Because of the flatness of the virtual experience, we reach for and react to any form of attention, even negative attention, because it comes closest to approximating true connection. It’s not surprising that these spaces produce behaviors that, like a maladjusted child’s, rely on attracting negative attention as a means of feeling something.

Of course, it is the adults who are most afflicted with malignant social immaturity who are the ones to become powerful politicians of the information age. Do not fret over it. This is normal for America. This country has always been fascinated with exploring the extremes, and yet, it's never fully given in to any one empire-ending scheme.

> American politics have always had politics have always had a strong element of the berserk. The dominant story Americans tend to learn in school about the nation does not exactly play that up. Our basic folktale is of an unbroken constitutional republic that through successive reforms grows more perfect but never suffered the collapse into dictatorship, fascism, or reign-of-terror violence of so many other countries. But the truth is far less tidy: we are a nation that, from one perspective, careens from episode to episode of mania, religious fervor, mass racist violence, and unrest.

The present-day America is simply an urban landscape overcrowded with blinking neon signs.

> We have a country full of megaphones, a crushing wall of sound, the swirling lights of a 24/7 casino blinking at us, all part of a system minutely engineered to take our attention away from us for profit. Under these conditions anything resembling democratic deliberation seems not only impossible but increasingly absurd, like trying to meditate in a strip club. The promise of the information age was unparalleled access to every single last bit of human knowledge at every moment, and the reality is a collective civic mental life that permanently teeters on the edge of madness.

Amidst all these blinking neon signs, American citizens are having trouble navigating the country's way out of this mess. They are hopelessly distracted and utterly exhausted.

> Hey sorry I missed your text, I am processing a non-stop 24/7 onslaught of information with a brain designed to eat berries in a cave.

However, every generation comes up with outliers, a set of people with remarkable brains, capable of making sense of and clearing out the mess the rest of us have made. Nowadays, the mess is centered around social media platforms like Facebook, Twitter, and TikTok.

> Those platforms are simply dynamic attention auctions where eyeballs are sold to advertisers based on which content makes you stop scrolling.

It's time to stop giving ad dollars to the algorithm. Without a revenue stream, it will die down, and people will wake up from their collective hallucination of manufactured rage. This should steer America clear of the cliff it's been heading toward. Its citizenry might just wake up from its algorithmically-induced coma and get their country back on track.
