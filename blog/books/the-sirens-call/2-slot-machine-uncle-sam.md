# 2. The Slot Machine and Uncle Sam

Different people pay attention to different things.

> There is no straightforward answer to the simple and very fraught (not to mention wildly lucrative) question: What will people pay attention to? And like cuisine, it’s the product of a million variables—history, culture, societal institutions. The boundless variety of the tastes of the human mind is beautiful, as is our capacity to pay deep attention to things that other people might find punishingly boring. There are infinite possibilities still out there, and what makes one individual at one moment pay sustained attention to something in particular is unknowable.

Capturing the attention of a person on a deep level is hard work, so companies have settled for the lowest common denominator to make their quick buck. Attention-seeking capitalists prey on things fundamental to human survival in order to wrestle our attention away and direct it toward their products. This is why there are so many sugary foods on supermarket shelves. Food journalist Michael Moss explains that our survival instinct draws us to sugar.

> Sugar, Moss says, provides us with that “deep biological satisfaction of doing something vital for our survival.”

Things that try too hard to get our attention are probably things that don't deserve it.

> When we associate clickbait—like tabloid daily papers with their crass, blaring covers—with cheap, low-quality information, it’s because we intuit that what they’re targeting is the low-hanging fruit, the easy parts of our attention. They’re operating on that twitchy part of our brain that’s searching for novel stimuli: threats, food, sex.

It's not so easy to grab the attention of a busy person. When we are told to pay attention to something, it's hard for something else to grab our attention from that task.

> Our ability to shut out nonrelevant stimuli while focusing our attention is so powerful that in all kinds of different experimental situations people will literally fail to see or hear extremely bizarre and often downright comedic stimuli right in front of them. The most famous of these is the invisible gorilla test.

There is more than one kind of blindness. Apart from the typical kind of blindness caused by lacking eyesight, there's also inattentional blindness: we see the entire picture in front of us but don't process it in its entirety—only the parts we pay attention to. If we focus intently on a part of the picture, we won't see other parts, even if they include bizarre, attention-grabbing elements.

> Experimental subjects watch a video of three people in white shirts and three people in black shirts all circulating and passing a basketball back and forth. The white shirts pass back and forth to each other as they circulate among the black shirts, who are also passing a basketball among themselves. It’s a chaotic scene. Experimental subjects are told to pay close attention to those in the white shirts and count how many times they pass the ball. To do this, you have to focus intently on just the participants in the white shirts and ignore the activity of those in black shirts. After thirty seconds of watching them pass it back and forth, about half the participants can correctly name the number of passes. But what they can’t recall—because they literally didn’t see it—was the fact that in the middle of the exercise a person dressed in a full gorilla costume walked out into the middle of the group, beat his chest, flexed, and walked away.

When it gets hard to focus on the road, we lower the volume of the stereo so we can see the road better, so we don't have to fight for the focus as hard.

> Higher perceptual loads make us more susceptible to a kind of tunnel vision.

Inattentional blindness is a trade-off for intentional focus. Trade-offs like this abound in nature. For example, as a consequence of human evolution, we had to trade having helpless, slow-developing infants for the ability to walk upright and having large brains.

> Human infants are considerably more helpless than lots of other infants. Nearly all mammals, from foals to piglets, can, for instance, walk far earlier in their infancy than human babies. If you’ve ever watched piglets or puppies fight to nurse, you know mammalian moms aren’t exactly lovingly attending to their litters the way a human mother puts her child to her breast. This is the by-product of two massively important evolutionary developments that famously distinguish our species: large brains and walking upright. In order to walk upright, the hips and birth canal of women had to narrow, while the brain of the fetus kept getting larger.

So, humans evolved to give birth earlier, when the baby's brain isn't as large yet to cause problems for the mother.

> Women who gave birth earlier, when the infant’s brain and head were still relatively small and supple, fared better and lived to have more children. Natural selection consequently favoured earlier births.
