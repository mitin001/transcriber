# Appendix G. More on Semiconductors

A diode is made by stacking two types of doped silicon: one doped with phosphorus (where each phosphorus atom has a free electron, N-type) and one doped with boron (where each boron atom has a hole that can accept a free electron, P-type). If we apply voltage across the diode such that the N end is more negative and P end is more positive, then electrons will flow across the diode, and so it will conduct electricity. However, if we switch the polarity of the applied voltage, we will force the free electrons from the N-type silicon to fill the holes in the P-type silicon, and so there will be a large depletion zone in the middle of the diode, which will prevent the flow of current across it.

![17505238006768958812520704105134](https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/9df18b4b-f521-4607-b82b-d756f518f0a3)

Transistors control the flow of current across them by exploiting this property of a diode. When current is applied to the capacitive gate of a transistor, the negative voltage is applied to the P silicon near it whereas the more positive voltage is at the N silicon surrounding it. This creates a depletion region in the silicon, which prevents current from flowing across it.

![17505237736311221542556096056228](https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/f3a0f0c3-2c4d-45f4-93f6-db34f6b9c57d)

The bigger the depletion region, the higher the resistance to current. For this reason, a transistor can also act as a voltage-controlled resistor.
