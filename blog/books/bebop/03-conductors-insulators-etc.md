# 3. Conductors, Insulators, and Other Stuff

We know from mechanics that a force of 1 newton accelerates an object weighing 1 kg in such a way that its velocity increases by 1 m/s every second. That is, 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². A joule of energy can be defined in terms of a newton: 1 J = 1 N·m. A watt of power can be defined in terms of a joule or amp and volt: 1 W = 1 J/s = 1 A·V. A volt can be defined in terms of an amp and ohm: 1 V = 1 A·Ω. Notice how we're capitalizing some unit abbreviations and not the others. The rule is that if the unit is named after a person, we capitalize its abbreviation. We don't capitalize units when spelling them out, regardless of whether they were named after people or not.

> In the case of an SI unit that is derived from the proper name of a person, the fi rst (often only) letter of its symbol must be in uppercase (V, A, W, Hz, etc.). By comparison, when this type of SI unit is spelled out in English, it should always begin with a lowercase letter (volt, amp, watt, hertz, etc.)

We're talking in terms of volts, amps, ohms, and watts because they're useful units in which to refer to electric events. We can't just talk in terms of particles; we need a way to refer to them in bulk. True, if we connect two objects, one of which has fewer electrons than the other, the excess electrons from one will rush toward the other. But it would be billions of billions of them between just two terminals of a small 1.5V battery.

> An amp corresponds to approximately 6,250,000,000,000,000,000 electrons per second flowing past a given point in an electrical circuit.

Metals resist the flow of electrons the least: their atoms do not require as much energy to exchange electrons. This makes metals ideal conductors of electricity.

> If a piece of copper wire is used to connect a source with an excess of electrons to a target with too few electrons, the wire will conduct electrons between them.

However, any material will conduct electricity if there is enough energy to overcome its resistance to electric current.

> Everything conducts if presented with a sufficiently powerful electric potential. For example, if you don a pair of rubber boots and then fly a kite in a thunderstorm, your rubber boots won’t save you when the lightning comes racing down the kite string.

While it takes the energy of a lightning to create an electric current through rubber, it's enough to simply touch a copper wire to an object with excess electric charge to get it an electric current to flow through it. The electric current will start flowing through the positive end of the wire almost as soon as it starts through the negative end—on the order of the speed of light.

> For a copper wire isolated in a vacuum, the speed of a signal propagating through the wire is only fractionally less than the speed of light. However, the speed of a signal is modified by a variety of factors, including any materials surrounding or near the conductor. Signal speeds in electronic circuits vary, but are typically in the vicinity of half the speed of light.

It is not to say that the electron entering one end of the wire will exit its other end in the time it would take it to travel through the wire at half the speed of light. Only that an electron entering one end of the wire will cause a chain reaction through the wire such that an electron at the other end of the wire will exit, and this chain reaction will take the time it would take a particle to travel the length of the wire at half the speed of light.

> As an electron jumps from the negative source into the wire, it “pushes” (repels) the nearest electron in the wire out of the way. This electron in turn pushes another, and the effect ripples down the wire until an electron at the far end of the wire is ejected into the more positive target.

If we traced an electron moving through the wire, it would take a much longer time for it to reach the other end than for the electric potential to propagate and start affecting electrons at the other end. A propagation of the electric field is a much faster process than the journey of an electron jumping through atoms.

> An individual electron can take a surprisingly long time to migrate from one end of the wire to the other. However, the time between an electron entering one end of the wire and causing an equivalent electron to be ejected from the other end is extremely fast.

A movement of water molecules through buckets and pipes in air is similar to the movement of electrons through conductors. A force of gravity pushing on water is like electric potential causing electrons to move to the area of positively charge. The higher the pressure, the higher the water flow. That is, the higher the voltage, the higher the current. The thinner the pipe, the lower the water flow. That is, the higher the resistance, the lower the current.
