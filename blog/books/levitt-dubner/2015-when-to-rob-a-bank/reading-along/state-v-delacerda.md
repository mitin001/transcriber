# [State v. Delacerda](https://www.cetient.com/case/state-v-delacerda-2621218)

During a routine bar check, the police caught Ramon Delacerda holding a bag of cocaine in the bar's public restroom. Delacerda asked for the cocaine to be deemed inadmissible as evidence because it was collected in a restroom.

> This appeal challenges the denial of a motion to suppress evidence in a prosecution for possession of a controlled substance. The evidence was gained when police officers forcibly entered a public restroom that was occupied by Ramon Delacerda and a second man, who attempted to hold the door shut against the police. The district court denied Delacerda's suppression motion.

Being in a restroom affords one a reasonable expectation of privacy, Delacerda's lawyer seems to think based on his understanding of the Fourth Amendment.

> After Delacerda was charged with possession of a controlled substance, Idaho Code § 37-2732(c), he filed a motion to suppress the evidence seized in the restroom. Delacerda asserted that the police officers' forcible entry of the restroom violated the Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article I, section 17 of the Idaho State Constitution. The district court denied the motion, holding that Delacerda had no reasonable expectation of privacy in the public restroom.

The court's decision seems to be at odds with the Fourth Amendment until we consider how it interprets this law.

> The Fourth Amendment and Article I, section 17 of the Idaho Constitution, guarantee freedom from unreasonable searches. These constitutional safeguards protect an individual's legitimate expectation of privacy, which has been defined as a subjective expectation of privacy that society is prepared to recognize as reasonable.

The court's interpretation of the Fourth Amendment rests on precedent. In an earlier case, an American court established that there's a difference between a reasonable and a subjective expectation of privacy. The Idaho Court of Appeals quotes a relevant portion of this case in its opinion of denial of the defendant's evidence suppression motion.

> A legitimate expectation of privacy "means more than a subjective expectation of not being discovered."

It quotes yet another case that pointed to a contradiction between an expectation of privacy in a public place.

> There can be no reasonable expectation of privacy in "[w]hat a person knowingly exposes to the public."

Even one's home can be construed by the court to be a public place if the inside of the home can be observed from the public street.

> Even activity within the home, to which the Fourth Amendment affords the greatest sanctity ... is not protected against police observation if the occupants of the home have done nothing to shield their activity from the view of persons who look from vantage points that are open to the public.

When the expectation of privacy of a defendant is up to debate, the court looks into the defendant's actions to determine if they took any actions aimed at protecting their privacy.

> In passing upon a claim of unreasonable governmental intrusion, we "examine the citizen's efforts to protect his own privacy from observation by the general public."

If you want the Fourth Amendment protections to extend to your case, you have to prove to the court that you took steps to protect your privacy. The law doesn't protect you automatically.

> The burden of proving a legitimate expectation of privacy lies with the defendant.

If you're in a stall of a public restroom, it is reasonable for you to expect privacy. Simply getting into a stall and locking the door is enough for any court to determine that you've taken adequate steps to qualify for the Fourth Amendment protections.

> This Court has previously held that a person has a reasonable expectation of privacy in the stall of a public restroom.

Masturbating in a public restroom is legal, the court decided in a case cited here, as long as it happens inside a locked stall (even if people can see you through the wide gap between the doors). Meanwhile, masturbating in a private home is illegal if people can see you doing it from the street (unless you've taken steps to prevent them from seeing you, even if those steps ultimately proved to be futile).

> The defendant was arrested for obscene conduct after an officer observed him, through a four-inch hole in a stall partition, masturbating in a public restroom. This Court determined that Limberhand had a legitimate expectation of privacy in the restroom stall notwithstanding the existence of the hole. The present case differs markedly from Limberhand, however, for here there was no stall, divider, or visual barrier of any kind, nor was there a lock on the restroom door that would allow a user to prevent entry by other persons.

Whereas occupying a locked stall inside a public restroom gives you a reasonable expectation of privacy (no matter the width of the gaps between the doors), occupying an unlocked public restroom itself does not give you a reasonable expectation of privacy.

> In the present case, the restroom was equipped with both a urinal and a toilet, thus permitting use by two people simultaneously, but with nothing to protect one user from the view of the other or from the view of anyone else who might enter the restroom. Given this visual openness of the restroom interior, and the lack of any lock to exclude others while the restroom was in use, an occupant could not reasonably expect privacy, even when using the restroom for its traditional purpose. Delacerda asserts, however, that he took steps to assure privacy by having the other man hold the door shut and thereby created a subjective expectation of privacy that should be viewed as objectively reasonable. He argues that when a person wishes to use a public restroom that provides no means to protect privacy, and therefore asks a companion to hold the door closed, the person should be deemed to possess the same expectation of privacy as if the door were locked. We do not necessarily reject Delacerda's premise that an individual in that circumstance would have a protected privacy interest; the flaw in Delacerda's argument is that the evidence does not show that circumstance here. Nothing in the record indicates that Delacerda himself took any steps to protect his privacy. There is no evidence that Delacerda asked the other man in the restroom to hold the door shut or was relying upon him to do so.

The man appeared to hold the door to the restroom to prevent the cops from entering it out of his own free will. Had Delacerda asked him to do so, the court might've found their evidence against him (the cocaine) inadmissible. Even if you're not trying to cover up a crime, always take steps to protect your privacy.
