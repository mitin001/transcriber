# 5. What Makes a Perfect Parent?

> The risks that scare people and the risks that kill people are very different.

This observation was made by Peter Sandman, the risk communications consultant.

> Sandman offered a comparison between mad-cow disease (a superscary but exceedingly rare threat) and the spread of food-borne pathogens in the average home kitchen (exceedingly common but somehow not very scary). “Risks that you control are much less a source of outrage than risks that are out of your control,” Sandman said. “In the case of mad-cow, it feels like it’s beyond my control. I can’t tell if my meat has prions in it or not. I can’t see it, I can’t smell it. Whereas dirt in my own kitchen is very much in my own control. I can clean my sponges. I can clean the floor.” Sandman’s “control” principle might also explain why most people are more scared of flying in an airplane than driving a car. Their thinking goes like this: since I control the car, I am the one keeping myself safe; since I have no control of the airplane, I am at the mercy of myriad external factors.

This is also the reason people are slow to adopt [self-driving cars], regardless of their safety statistics. You can point out all you want that letting a human drive is n times more likely to result in an accident, but the human will always say that this average doesn't apply to him. He's above average. Most people, far more than 50%, view themselves to be [above average]. Biases like these make people easy to [manipulate]. There are hoards of so-called experts who prey on human vulnerabilities to make a name for themselves.

> An expert whose argument reeks of restraint or nuance often doesn’t get much attention. An expert must be bold if he hopes to alchemize his homespun theory into conventional wisdom. His best chance of doing so is to engage the public’s emotions, for emotion is the enemy of rational argument. And as emotions go, one of them—fear—is more potent than the rest. The superpredator, Iraqi weapons of mass destruction, mad-cow disease, crib death: how can we fail to heed the expert’s advice on these horrors when, like that mean uncle telling too-scary stories to too-young children, he has reduced us to quivers?

People are especially vulnerable to getting scammed when the so-called expert makes a scary pronouncement about their children.

> No one is more susceptible to an expert’s fearmongering than a parent.

This is why parents let Republicans [burn books] and attack the schools. Their [anti-trans propaganda] is working because they have convinced parents that it could happen to their children. In their propaganda-fueled nightmares, the child will come home forever changed by gender reassignment surgery because the evil liberals running the schools will have convinced him he was trans, and no one will have even bothered to tell the parent until it was too late. Republicans use children to scare people into electing them. Car-seat manufacturers use children to scare lawmakers into passing laws prohibiting children from riding in cars unless they're strapped to their products, regardless of the fact that statistically they're no safer than seatbelts. Parents will go out of their way to ensure the safety of their child, even though most safety measures that so-called experts are selling them are nonsense. A parent who doesn't listen to parental advice can still be a good parent. Consider the observation that children who are spanked perform better on standardized tests than many other classes of children, even those who have been adopted.

> If an adopted child is prone to lower test scores, a spanked child is not. This may seem surprising—not because spanking itself is necessarily detrimental but because, conventionally speaking, spanking is considered an unenlightened practice. We might therefore assume that parents who spank are unenlightened in other ways. Perhaps that isn’t the case at all. Or perhaps there is a different spanking story to be told. Remember, the ECLS survey included direct interviews with the children’s parents. So a parent would have to sit knee to knee with a government researcher and admit to spanking his child. This would suggest that a parent who does so is either unenlightened or—more interestingly—congenitally honest. It may be that honesty is more important to good parenting than spanking is to bad parenting.

Parents who still spank their children are unlikely to be getting their parental advice from any experts because not spanking one's child is among the first things any parental advice giver will mention. And yet, their children still turn out fine, perhaps their parents are honest. If they're honest enough to admit to spanking their child to an interviewer, honesty must take a high priority in their lives, and this virtue is more important to good parenting than following any nonsense safety measure the fearmongerer is trying to sell them.
