# 3. Why Do Drug Dealers Still Live with Their Moms?

If a pregnant woman is seeking an abortion, she has a good reason. If you deny her an abortion, her child will grow up with adversity and may turn to crime to survive. This is why there was such [a decrease in crime following the legalization of abortion] in the United States. Pro-life advocates have another solution to reducing the pool of criminals in society. Sure, the children of adversity are our future criminals, but many of the immigrants are criminals, too, they will say. Immigrants bring adversity, and where there is adversity, there is crime. Instead of aborting fetuses, why not just go after immigrants?

> In the United States, gangs have traditionally been a sort of halfway house for recent immigrants. In the 1920s, Chicago alone had more than 1,300 street gangs, catering to every ethnic, political, and criminal leaning imaginable.

Except nowadays, immigrants have a much easier time finding work that is not gang-related. The Trump administration doesn't raid gangs. His ICE agents [raid] schools, churches, and Home Depots. Gangs are the provenance of the drug trade, which has become very much domesticated in this country. It is big business, something America respects so much that it has legalized drug gangs, as long as they hail from the pharmaceutical industry. Doctors get kickbacks from Purdue when they [overprescribe] OxyContin, addicting people to it by the thousands. OxyContin is like crack in many respects, the most important of which is that both of these drugs ravage entire communities. These drugs kill their users, and the stray bullets from the gang wars over them kill everyone else. Gang lords like Purdue or the mafia bosses may wish there was no gang violence, but with the drugs this potent, the violence is inevitable. To illustrate this, the freakonomists give the readers the perspective of the drug gang leader named J. T.

> A crack boss didn’t have as much control over his subordinates as he would have liked. That’s because they had different incentives. For J. T., violence was a distraction from the business at hand; he would have preferred that his members never fired a single gunshot. For a foot soldier, however, violence served a purpose. One of the few ways that a foot soldier could distinguish himself—and advance in the tournament—was by proving his mettle for violence. A killer was respected, feared, talked about. A foot soldier’s incentive was to make a name for himself; J. T.’s incentive was, in effect, to keep the foot soldiers from doing so.

Adversity begets crime. Desperation begets violence. A low-level drug dealer needs to get the attention of the streets to make it out of poverty. A beginner journalist could make a name for himself by finding a bombshell and breaking an explosive story before anyone else reports it. He gets ahead from under his competition. A drug dealer makes a name for himself by literally killing his competition.

> An editorial assistant earning $22,000 at a Manhattan publishing house, an unpaid high-school quarterback, and a teenage crack dealer earning $3.30 an hour are all playing the same game, a game that is best viewed as a tournament. The rules of a tournament are straightforward. You must start at the bottom to have a shot at the top.

The reason entry-level drug dealers make so little on a product so lucrative is because there are so many of them.

> When there are a lot of people willing and able to do a job, that job generally doesn’t pay well. This is one of four meaningful factors that determine a wage. The others are the specialized skills a job requires, the unpleasantness of a job, and the demand for services that the job fulfills.

There are so many desperate impoverished people turning to drug dealing that the demand for the product doesn't matter. It doesn't matter that their job requires expert-level discretion and communication skills. It doesn't matter that the job is highly unpleasant and dangerous, with a high risk of getting killed or caught. The sheer supply of people willing to do the job vastly diminishes the significance of these factors and reduces the wage to peanuts. Prostitution is similar. It is another low-paying, high-demand job.

> Let’s just say that an architect is more likely to hire a prostitute than vice versa.

Sex and drugs are dominant forces in society because they are addicting. To sell these is to profit from human weaknesses, [exploit human vulnerabilities]. Make a new kind of drug, and you will be rich. [Tech companies] benefiting from society's addiction to the phone constantly reinvent crack in the guise of a hot new app: Facebook, YouTube, TikTok, etc. Crack itself was a reinvention of another drug, cocaine. It was the version of cocaine one could sell at scale on the streets.

> Cocaine had never been a big seller in the ghetto: it was too expensive. But that was before the invention of crack. This new product was ideal for a low-income, street-level customer. Because it required such a tiny amount of pure cocaine, one hit of crack cost only a few dollars. Its powerful high reached the brain in just a few seconds—and then faded fast, sending the user back for more. From the outset, crack was bound to be a huge success.

Making crack was a way to safely raise the potency of distilled cocaine. Baking soda cracks when burned. That's where crack got its name.

> Mixing powdered cocaine in a saucepan with baking soda and water, and then cooking off the liquid, produced tiny rocks of smokeable cocaine. It came to be called crack for the crackling sound the baking soda made when it was burned.
