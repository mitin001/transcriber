# Epilogue: monkeys are people too

Keith Chen conducted experiments on capuchin monkeys. He arranged for them to meet two researchers: one holding two grapes and another holding just one grape. The two-grape researcher didn't always give the monkeys both grapes, whereas the one-grape researcher sometimes pulled a second grape out of his pocket and gave both to the monkeys.

> Once the monkeys figured out that the two-grape researcher sometimes withheld the second grape and that the one-grape researcher sometimes added a bonus grape, the monkeys strongly preferred the one-grape researcher. A rational monkey wouldn’t have cared, but these irrational monkeys suffered from what psychologists call “**loss aversion**.” They behaved as if the pain from losing a grape was greater than the pleasure from gaining one.

Humans behave exactly the same as lab monkeys in this regard. They also suffer from loss aversion.

> People make the same kind of irrational decisions at a nearly identical rate. The data generated by the capuchin monkeys, Chen says, “make them statistically indistinguishable from most stock-market investors.”

The extent of the agony from unexpected disappointment is greater than the extent of the pleasure derived from unexpected reward. The highs are not as high, but the lows are too low. Perhaps this is why negative experiences are more memorable. Maybe our lives, and the lives of lab animals, are already filled with so much joy that our brains already overflow with dopamine, and adding a little more [doesn't feel like much]. Maybe the baseline of our experiences is too high already, and that's why there's only room for the downward swing. We're desensitized to pleasure. Meanwhile, discomfort is such an uncommon feeling that when we experience it, the agony is overwhelming to us, and we learn to avoid it at all costs. That's the tragedy of a pleasure-filled life.
