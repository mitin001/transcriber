Over the past fifty years, medicine has come a long way in its understanding of heart disease, especially compared to cancer, the cure for which remains largely an intractable problem.

> Many people who in previous generations would have died from heart disease are now living long enough to die from cancer instead.

Early detection of cancer is not the answer. Universal screening for cancer will lead to so many false positives it will likely overwhelm the healthcare system that is already strained as it is.

> Although health-care advocates may urge universal screening for all sorts of maladies, the reality is that the system would be overwhelmed by false positives and the sick would be crowded out.

We don't have the luxury of flooding the population with tests. When studying population health, we have to look at the trends presented only by the sickest subset of the population, those who couldn't be turned away even by the scarcely rationed healthcare system. Unfortunately, the poor health habits of the population are sure to keep this cohort of patients in large numbers. For example, Ugandan babies born in May 2010 will be significantly less healthy than babies born in other months.

> If you know someone in southeastern Uganda who is having a baby next year, you should hope with all your heart that the baby isn’t born in May. If so, it will be roughly 20 percent more likely to have visual, hearing, or learning disabilities as an adult. Three years from now, however, May would be a fine month to have a baby. But the danger will have only shifted, not disappeared; April would now be the cruelest month.

> Islam calls for a daytime fast from food and drink for the entire month of Ramadan. Most Muslim women participate even while pregnant.

If the Ramadan fast fell on the first month of pregnancy, the effects will be particularly pronounced.

> Babies that were in utero during Ramadan are more likely to exhibit developmental aftereffects. The magnitude of these effects depends on which month of gestation the baby is in when Ramadan falls. The effects are strongest when fasting coincides with the first month of pregnancy.

Astrologers look for relationships between a person's birthday and their fate but never explain those relationships with anything more useful than their astronomical allegories. This is where Levitt and Dubner step in. When they see a birthday bulge, they ask questions that lead them to satisfactory explanations. If there's a bulge around developmental disabilities for babies born in a certain month, it's not because they were born Gemini, but because their mothers were participating in a cultural tradition that required them to fast in the first month of their pregnancy. Similarly, if there's a bulge around athletic ability in the first three months of the year, it's because these people had extra practice time as children in their first year of playing the sport, which set the course for their entire athletic career.

> If you visit the locker room of a world-class soccer team early in the calendar year, you are more likely to interrupt a birthday celebration than if you arrive later in the year. A recent tally of the British national youth leagues, for instance, shows that fully half of the players were born between January and March, with the other half spread out over the nine remaining months. On a similar German team, 52 elite players were born between January and March, with just 4 players born between October and December.

A child born in January will have a whole year of development and practice on a child born in December. Which one of them will be more likely to qualify for the youth league?

> Most elite athletes begin playing their sports when they are quite young. Since youth sports are organized by age, the leagues naturally impose a cutoff birthdate. The youth soccer leagues in Europe, like many such leagues, use December 31 as the cutoff date. Imagine now that you coach in a league for seven-year-old boys and are assessing two players. The first one (his name is Jan) was born on January 1, while the second one (his name is Tomas) was born 364 days later, on December 31. So even though they are both technically seven-year-olds, Jan is a year older than Tomas—which, at this tender age, confers substantial advantages. Jan is likely to be bigger, faster, and more mature than Tomas.

There's nothing special about soccer that makes it attract birthday bulges. If you look at baseball, you'll find them too.

> Once you start to look, birthdate bulges are everywhere. Consider the case of Major League Baseball players. Most youth leagues in the United States have a July 31 cutoff date. As it turns out, a U.S.-born boy is roughly 50 percent more likely to make the majors if he is born in August instead of July. Unless you are a big, big believer in astrology, it is hard to argue that someone is 50 percent better at hitting a big-league curveball simply because he is a Leo rather than a Cancer.
