# Introduction

Levitt and Dubner begin their book by admitting that they are not macroeconomists. They quip that no one seems to be particularly good at being a macroeconomist, lest we wouldn't be finding ourselves in the mess of the financial crisis this often.

> The macroeconomy and its multitude of complex, moving parts is simply not our domain. After recent events, one might wonder if the macroeconomy is the domain of any economist. Most economists the public encounters are presented as oracles who can tell you, with alluring certainty, where the stock market or inflation or interest rates are heading. But as we’ve seen lately, such predictions are generally worthless. Economists have a hard enough time explaining the past, much less predicting the future.

Levitt and Dubner are macroeconomists. They focus on highlighting smaller, more manageable, and perhaps more useful problems in economics. For example, they teach us not to trust personal surveys. Economists and sociologists should observe people instead. There's a difference between **declared preferences** and **revealed preferences**.

> There is good reason to be skeptical of data from personal surveys. There is often a vast gulf between how people say they behave and how they actually behave.

There's similarly a caveat we should never forget when dealing with averages: the average is not the same as the mode, and the average case may actually not exist in practice.

> Some people may feel uneasy about reducing the vagaries of human behavior to cold numerical probabilities. Who among us wants to describe ourselves as “typical”? If, for instance, you added up all the women and men on the planet, you would find that, on average, the typical adult human being has one breast and one testicle—and yet how many people fit that description?

Levitt and Dubner conduct several case studies to illustrate their view of microeconomics. For example, they point out that dispensing advice like "you should walk home" to a drunk is not necessarily a good idea. Drunk walking is far more dangerous than drink driving.

> On a per-mile basis, a drunk walker is eight times more likely to get killed than a drunk driver. There’s one important caveat: a drunk walker isn’t likely to hurt or kill anyone other than her-or himself. That can’t be said of a drunk driver. In fatal accidents involving alcohol, 36 percent of the victims are either passengers, pedestrians, or other drivers. Still, even after factoring in the deaths of those innocents, walking drunk leads to five times as many deaths per mile as driving drunk.

The age of the automobile is a dangerous age. But we're still lucky to live in this age. Transportation scholar Eric Morris tells horror stories of what life was like before the invention of the automobile. City streets were overflowing with horse manure and even horse corpses.

> Street cleaners often waited for the corpses to putrefy so they could more easily be sawed into pieces and carted off.

There's variation in luck even among people who live in the same age. It's luckier to be born in the developed world than developing world. It's luckier to be born male than female. It's particularly unlucky to be born a female in the developing world.

> Many Indian parents express a strong “son preference.” Only 10 percent of Indian families with two sons want another child, whereas nearly 40 percent of families with two daughters want to try again. Giving birth to a baby boy is like giving birth to a 401(k) retirement fund. He will grow up to be a wage-earning man who can provide for his parents in their sunset years and, when the time comes, light the funeral pyre. Having a baby girl, meanwhile, means relabeling the retirement fund a dowry fund.

Many Indian women grow up feeling unwanted, and the problems don't stop there. Women in any society have to make up for the [shortcomings] of their men, but Indian women have it especially bad. The anatomical disadvantages of their men endangers their health.

> Indian women also run an outsize risk of unwanted pregnancy and sexually transmitted disease, including a high rate of HIV/AIDS. One cause is that Indian men’s condoms malfunction more than 15 percent of the time. Why such a high fail rate? According to the Indian Council of Medical Research, some 60 percent of Indian men have penises too small for the condoms manufactured to fit World Health Organization specs. That was the conclusion of a two-year study in which more than 1,000 Indian men had their penises measured and photographed by scientists. “The condom,” declared one of the researchers, “is not optimized for India.”
