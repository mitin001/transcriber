# 1. What Does It Mean to Think Like a Freak

Young people sometimes luck into finding a new solution to a long-unsolved problem because they [dare]. They don't yet have the experience to know that they're not special, that many people smarter than they are have already looked into the problem and couldn't find the solution. However, because so many people have developed the maturity to stop daring to solve the big problems, maybe all that's been missing is precisely the brute force of the number of people bold enough to tackle it. However, the trouble with the brute force approach to solving problems is that it wastes the time of almost everyone who attempts it. Brute force is inefficient. Don't fall into the trap of overestimating your luck. Most people would do better to stay away from big problems and instead throw themselves into the smaller ones.

> Solving problems is hard. If a given problem still exists, you can bet that a lot of people have already come along and failed to solve it. Easy problems evaporate; it is the hard ones that linger. Furthermore, it takes a lot of time to track down, organize, and analyze the data to answer even one small question well.

Finding the [cure for cancer] is a hard problem that's gone unsolved for far too long, but most people would do well to tackle the smaller problems associated with cancer prevention and screening. For example, one could figure out how much screening is optimal, i.e., sufficient to accomplish a significant rate of early detection without overstraining the healthcare system. The freakonomists paint a bleak picture when describing what an overstrained healthcare system looks like.

> The “worried well” crowd out the truly sick, wait times increase for everyone, and a massive share of the costs go to the final months of elderly patients’ lives, often without much real advantage.

Every decision comes with trade-offs.
