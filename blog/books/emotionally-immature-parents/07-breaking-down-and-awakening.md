# 7. Breaking Down and Awakening

Your brain is constantly processing information in the background. Sometimes this background processing comes into the foreground, demanding your attention. If you're suffering the grief of loss or a breakup, you can often focus on something else for short periods of time before the psychological pain steals your focus away. The brain is constantly working on repairing psychological trauma in the background, and if you have a deep-seated trauma from childhood, the brain will push it into the foreground from time to time.

> Painful symptoms like depression, anxiety, chronic tension, or not sleeping can all be signals that old strategies to rewrite reality have become unsustainable. These psychological and physical symptoms are a warning system, telling us that we need to get back in sync with who we are and how we really feel.

What the brain is demanding is a change of behavior. It has finished processing a traumatic event and arrived at the realization that you must change your behavior to finish the healing process. Stop playing the role engendered in your childhood, start focusing on your inner world, and strengthen the connection with your true self. Taking on a role plays into a healing fantasy, which was a child's idea of how to heal. The mature, adult brain has a better solution for you, and all you must do is listen to it.

> People experience a breakdown when the pain of living in role-selves and healing fantasies begins to outweigh any potential benefits. Most psychological growth exposes some distressing truths about what we’ve been doing with our lives. Psychotherapy and the like are aids to help us become aware of truths we already know in our bones. When you’re going through a breakdown, a good question to ask is what is actually breaking down. We usually think it’s our self. But what’s typically happening is that our struggle to deny our emotional truth is breaking down. Emotional distress is a signal that it’s getting harder to remain emotionally unconscious. It means we’re about to discover our true selves.

Psychotherapy won't give you the answer for what to do to heal your emotional trauma. The answer is simply to make a change and listen to yourself for how that change makes you feel. Your inner self knows exactly what is best for you. Psychotherapy is simply a set of techniques to try to get you in touch with your inner self.

> Your true self wants you to see what’s really going on. It tries to wake you up because it wants you to stop believing that your emotionally immature parents knew what was best for you and that creating a role-self is better than being who you really are. It knows better than to let a fantasy run your life.

By listening to his inner self, one man discovered that he wasn't taught the right things about how to be successful. He had been taught that you must work hard, and sooner or later, someone will notice. After getting some life experience, his brain was finally mature enough to realize that success was more about negotiating with powerful figures.

> His code of not speaking up for himself often resulted in other people being put ahead of him. In addition, others often took advantage of his talents without reciprocating. He saw that his healing fantasy, in which he hoped authority figures would spontaneously recognize his potential, wasn’t coming to fruition. So he decided to develop a new value of going after what he wanted. He started actively seeking opportunities and laying claim to them. Considering a job change, he said, “In the past, I would have been reluctant to do this for myself, but now I’m not.” He finally saw himself as worthy of standing up for and investing in.

One technique psychotherapy has for preventing a person from ruminating on negative feelings and instead focusing them on healing is journaling. It helps to frame the exploration of what your brain is trying to tell you in terms of roles and healing fantasies. Which of your behaviors aren't really a part of you? It's time to abandon those behaviors.

> Write a short personality description of someone in your life who makes you feel nervous or small. Next, think about how you behave around that person, and then write a short description of the role-self you’ve been playing with the person. See if you can spot a healing fantasy that might be driving you to seek this person’s acceptance at all costs.

Exercises like these can help focus away from the symptoms of psychological distress.

> Do this exercise anytime you’re feeling especially anxious or in a down mood. At those times, ask yourself whether you might be harboring some hidden feelings. Consider the times when you feel worst and see if they’re related to thinking about a certain person. (In my experience, the two feelings people seem most reluctant to admit are being afraid of someone or not liking someone.)

Imagine you've met a person you want to love you, but they don't. But you desperately need them to fill the void left over from your childhood in lieu of having an emotionally mature parent. In your desperation to get them to rescue you from that void, you allow them to abuse you. Perhaps you let them degrade you sexually. Perhaps you feel like you have to put up with their insults. Psychotherapy may give you permission to admit that you don't actually like this person and don't need them in your life. Intimacy is about getting to know someone's inner world from the perspective of your inner world. If you haven't built up your inner world, you won't be able to connect with the right person on that level and instead find people who only want to use you.

> Relationship problems present a huge opportunity to wake up. Given that we tend to play out painful patterns learned in childhood in our significant adult relationships, it isn’t surprising that so many people come for therapy because of relationship issues. And because intimate adult relationships are so emotionally arousing, they tend to activate unresolved issues about not getting our emotional needs met. We often project issues about our parents onto our partners.

Once you've realized that you don't actually like your abusive partner, leave them. If they've already left you, don't come back to them. Don't even try to talk to them.

> Some people think it’s necessary to confront the other person to get a true resolution, but I believe this is often counterproductive and provokes too much anxiety. Disclosing feelings too soon may flood you with unnecessary anxiety—not to mention risking a backlash—when you’re just beginning to get in touch with your true feelings. You can always talk to the person later if you wish, but first you need to regain your ability to speak your feelings to yourself. Just to be clear, what helps isn’t telling the other person; it’s knowing what you really feel. Simply admitting your true feelings and stating them out loud can make a huge difference in regaining your emotional peace.

Talking to abusive people is a step back. You may desperately want an explanation for why some piece of shit ghosted you, but don't show this desperation to them. Don't text them begging to tell you why they threw you away like trash after using you. This behavior is you playing the role of a child desperate for a loving parent, and it's no longer appropriate. It's not who you are. Simply move on and discover who you are.

> The true self will find ways to express itself, even in the face of efforts to play a role or live out a healing fantasy. When people have ignored their true self for too long, they may develop psychological symptoms. Waking up to the needs of the true self can initially feel like breaking down. Panic, anger, and depression are just a few symptoms that may signal an emotional awakening to better self-care and healthier values. When people process their childhood issues and wake up to their strengths, they gain the confidence to start living from their true self.
