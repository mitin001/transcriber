# 5. How Different Children React to Emotionally Immature Parenting

A potential mate on a dating site asks you, "What are you looking for?" You decide to be honest, if only just this once, so you respond that you're looking for a daddy. You explain that you never felt love from your father, and even though you can't turn back time, you're at least looking for a partner to fill that void now that you're an adult. Do not expect anyone reading such a revelation ever to text you back.

> We usually have no idea that we’re trying to foist a healing fantasy on someone, but it can be seen in the little tests of love we put people through. It’s easier for an outsider to see how unrealistic the fantasy is. Successful marital therapy often involves exposing how people’s healing fantasies try to force their partners to give them the loving childhood they always wished for.

Do not force anyone to play the role of your parent, no matter how bad your parents were to you. You cannot build a relationship with role-playing. You can only do so by being yourself and inviting the other person into your [inner world]. Role-playing, on the other hand, invites a twisted, duplicitous reality where nothing matters more than keeping up the roles. Facts don't matter. You can never convince a role-player of anything with facts. A woman growing up in a family of role-players decided to stop giving in to their nonsense, so the family turned against her by inventing an unfounded accusation that she had sexually assaulted her brother. They incorporated this fiction into their family lore and refused to believe anything else.

> The roles her parents and brother played as rescuers and hapless victim were too well-established for the facts to have a bearing.

A role-player gives all their energy to keeping up appearances. Externalizers are particularly stubborn role-players. A role is their entire life. Playing a role is their only interest. To everything else, they say, "Not my job." Everything in the world is a prop in their theater, and since they're the actor, other people must tend to their props. To an externalizer, fixing things is always someone else's job.

> Looking to others to improve things: “What should someone else do to make things better?”

They will impulsively take an action without thinking through the consequences. When something piques their interest, their mind immediately builds a magical world where the endeavor they're thinking up goes perfectly smoothly, with no difficulties in sight. Suppose they start talking to someone out of state on a dating site. In this case, they will immediately book an expensive, non-refundable trip to come meet them in person. When the dating-site flake proceeds to promptly ghost them, they will be utterly distraught. To the point where they won't even go on the prepaid trip alone to make the most of the cash they have spent. They will just let it all go to waste. Next time they chat up a flaky out-of-state stranger on the same dating site, the same story will repeat. Again and again. Just imagine how much money this kind of person will lose if a full-blown scammer gets hold of them.

> Blaming circumstances. Getting others involved in their problems. Denying or escaping reality to feel better.

Externalizers prefer not to live in our shared reality. They build a world of their own in their heads where there is no room for difficulties. Of course, when they inevitably bump up against our shared reality with its difficulties, they can't make sense of them, so they blame supernatural forces for those. Since they actually live _here_, not in the fairy tales of their minds, they will have to confront difficulties. But since there cannot be difficulties in an externalizer's world, it must be something not of this world that's responsible for them. Cue supernatural beings, fortunetellers, and the chakras.
