# Wireless Transmission

Why can't a tower in Amsterdam receive a microwave signal from Seattle? Because microwaves travel in straight lines, and there's enough of a curvature of the Earth between these towers for the microwaves to be absorbed by it.

> Microwaves are directional: they travel in a straight line, so if the towers are too far apart, the earth will get in the way (think about a Seattle-to-Amsterdam link). Thus, repeaters are needed periodically. The higher the towers are, the farther apart they can be.

If there's an aquarium or shower between your computer and your 5 GHz access point, you will not get any signal. It will be absorbed by the water.

> At around 4 GHz, a new problem sets in: absorption by water. These waves are only a few centimeters long and are absorbed by rain.

The microwave spectrum is in short supply for its ubiquitous demand.

> Microwave communication is so widely used for long-distance telephone communication, mobile phones, television distribution, and other purposes that a severe shortage of spectrum has developed.
> 

Microwaves do not demand much land use.

> No right of way is needed to lay down cables. By buying a small plot of ground every 50 km and putting a microwave tower on it, one can bypass the telephone system entirely. This is how MCI managed to get started as a new long-distance telephone company so quickly.

Telephone companies spring out of abundance of infrastructure. If you have a way to propagate your signal across large distances, start a telecom. This is the origin story of Sprint.

> It was formed by the Southern Pacific Railroad, which already owned a large amount of right of way and just buried fiber next to the tracks.

Infrared light illustrates how the physics of electromagnetic radiation change as the waves get shorter. The shorter the wave, the more directional and shieldable it is.

> They do not pass through solid objects. (Try standing between your remote control and your television and see if it still works.) In general, as we go from long-wave radio toward visible light, the waves behave more and more like light and less and less like radio.
