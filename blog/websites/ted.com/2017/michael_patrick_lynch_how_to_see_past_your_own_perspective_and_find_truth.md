# [Michael Patrick Lynch: How to see past your own perspective and find truth](https://www.ted.com/talks/michael_patrick_lynch_how_to_see_past_your_own_perspective_and_find_truth)

Why do enablers of fascist movements parrot the lies of their founders? This is how they prove that the only thing that should matter is what the fascist authorities say, not what is true. This is them passing their litmus test.

> At the end of Orwell's "1984" the thought policeman O'Brien is torturing the protagonist Winston Smith into believing two plus two equals five. What O'Brien says is the point, is that he wants to convince Smith that whatever the party says is the truth, and the truth is whatever the party says. And what O'Brien knows is that once this thought is accepted, critical dissent is impossible. You can't speak truth to power if the power speaks truth by definition.

A fascist movement has a central dogma imposed by the top, and everyone under must fall in line and adopt this dogma. The Internet has advanced to the point where it is composed of basins that suck people into them. At the center of each basin is a dogma the members must repeat lest they be expelled from the group. No opinion is allowed that strays away from the dogma.

> We download prepackaged sets of facts and sort of shuffle them along the assembly line of social media.

If you try sharing anything that isn't relevant to or goes against the central dogma of the basin in which you swim, your post will get no attention or you will be reported to moderators who will kick you out of the thread, forum, or platform you dared to disrespect by expressing an original thought. As these dogmatic basins suck more people into them, bubbles form around them, and you aren't allowed to know more than what is in your bubble.

> Bubble-knowing means always being right. But daring to know, daring to understand, means risking the possibility that you could be wrong. It means risking the possibility that what you want and what's true are different things.

Lynch proposed we drop the concept of bubble-knowing and instead embrace **epistemic humility**.

> Knowing that you don't know it all.

If you see an opinion on the Internet that you disagree with, don't dismiss it simply because it goes against the dogma of your bubble. Practice epistemic humility.

> Seeing your worldview as open to improvement by the evidence and experience of others.

Unless the expressed opinion is simply an exercise in trolling, try to understand why the person expressing it crossed over into your bubble and brought a piece of another bubble's dogma along with them. Is it really a hand-me-down from some authority figure or is it the truth? To ask a question like this is to practice epistemic humility.

> It means seeing your knowledge as capable of enhancing or being enriched by what others contribute.

No human being is a waste of your time. Everyone can contribute to your understanding of the world. Everyone can teach you something new. This should not be a radical idea, but somehow it is.

> Our society is not particularly great at enhancing or encouraging that sort of humility. That's partly because, well, we tend to confuse arrogance and confidence. And it's partly because, well, you know, arrogance is just easier. It's just easier to think of yourself as knowing it all. It's just easier to think of yourself as having it all figured out. But that's another example of the bad faith towards the truth.

If the Internet is to develop further and get closer to our brains, we must learn to argue on it in good faith.

> We may yet one day realize the vision of having the internet in our brains. But if we want that to be liberating and not terrifying, if we want it to expand our understanding and not just our passive knowing, we need to remember that our perspectives, as wondrous, as beautiful as they are, are just that -- perspectives on one reality.
