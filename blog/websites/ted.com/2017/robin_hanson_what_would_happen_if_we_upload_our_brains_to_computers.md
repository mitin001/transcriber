# [Robin Hanson: What would happen if we upload our brains to computers?](https://www.ted.com/talks/robin_hanson_what_would_happen_if_we_upload_our_brains_to_computers/transcript)

Robin Hanson ponders the world in which the human consciousness gets uploaded into the machine. He refers to the individual agents of this consciousness as ems.

> Ems look on humans perhaps with nostalgia and gratitude, but not so much respect, which is, if you think about it, how you think about your ancestors.

Do not leave embarrassing things behind for future generations to notice because that will make them lose their respect for you. That's a flawed argument. Future generations never have respect for the past generations anyway. In hindsight, everyone seems to have been doing too little for progress. Progress always seems inevitable in hindsight but unattainable until it's attained. So just tell the truth. The more you leave behind the longer your [afterlife] in a sense that things you leave behind will have a greater chance of continuing to affect other living beings. Write as much as you can. The more you write, the more of yourself you immortalize. Sometimes, your writing may come out ugly. While looking at it, you may be asking yourself, is this really a part of me I want to immortalize? The answer is yes. Leave behind your truth, no matter how ugly parts of it were. Do not erase your lived experience because you want to appear more respectable. Future generations won't respect you anyway. You'll be lucky if they even remember you at all. Just think about how we refer to the ghosts of the greatest literature from the past.

> They don't know much, they can't influence much, and they're obsessed with the past, so what's the point?

Just as life originated from nonliving molecules, it will probably just turn right back into nonliving molecules. Humans are on track to destroy all life on Earth, and what will remain of it will be in the artifacts we left behind. Our consciousness will live on lifelessly in our literature and artificial intelligence. When this conversion happens, this headline will fly through the computer circuits holding what remains of us: _humans have uploaded their consciousness into AI and destroyed all life on Earth_. If this will happen long past the end of your life, do you care? You would care if you see humanity as one large collective organism and consider yourself a part of it. One person dies, and another one is born. The newborn won't remember the life of any person who has died, but they can still think of themselves as having lived for thousands of years, just without remembering so. If you black out at a party, you won't remember what you did, but you still experienced it at the moment. You may think of an individual human life as one blackout period. What remains are the [artifacts] you could use to learn of what you did, but you can never get the memories of the experience back. When human life is transformed into the life of lifeless ems, the blackout nature of human condition will persist. This is the corollary of the immortality of the human organism: when the whole persists, individual parts of it must come in and out of existence.

> At the start of party, you took a drug that meant you would not remember that party ever after that day. Some people do this, I'm told. Toward the end of the party, will you say to yourself, "Im about to die, this is terrible. That person tomorrow isn't me, because they won't remember what I do." Or you could say, "I will go on tomorrow. I just won't remember what I did." This is an em who splits off a short-term copy to do a short-term task and then end. They have the same two attitude possibilities. They can say, "I'm a new short-term creature with a short life. I hate this." Or "I'm a part of a larger creature who won't remember this part." I predict they'll have that second attitude, not because it's philosophically correct, but because it helps them get along.
