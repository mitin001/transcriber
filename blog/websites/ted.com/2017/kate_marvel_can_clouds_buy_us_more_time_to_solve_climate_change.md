# [Kate Marvel: Can clouds buy us more time to solve climate change?](https://www.ted.com/talks/kate_marvel_can_clouds_buy_us_more_time_to_solve_climate_change/transcript)

> We don't know for sure what the future holds. But we are sending our kids there, and they are never coming back.

Different climate models predict wildly different futures. Clouds are a big contributor to these discrepancies.

Low clouds have the opposite effect on climate change than the effect high clouds have.

> Low clouds are powerful sunshades, making the planet cooler. But that's not the only effect of clouds. Our planet has a temperature, and like anything with a temperature, it's giving off heat. We are radiating thermal energy away into space.

Whereas low clouds block some of the energy from the sun from reaching Earth, high clouds keep the energy from the Earth from radiating away into space.

> The earth is trying to cool itself off, and high clouds are getting in the way. The result is a very powerful greenhouse effect.

The high clouds are the ones contributing to the greenhouse effect.

> We've got low clouds that act like a sunshade, cooling the planet, and high clouds which act like a greenhouse, warming the planet.

Low clouds have a slightly more pronounced effect on climate change models.

> Right now, these two effects -- they don't cancel out. That sunshade -- it's a little bit more powerful.

We should brace ourselves for when the high clouds overtake them in this climatic tug of war. Or, perhaps, we should just relax, and let the lead low clouds already have widen even more, to become even more effective at reflecting the sunlight.

> If global warming triggers cloud changes that make for a less powerful greenhouse or a more effective sunshade, then that would enhance the cooling power of clouds. It would act in opposition to global warming, and that's what's happening in those climate models that project relatively muted warming. But climate models struggle with clouds, and this uncertainty -- it goes both ways. Clouds could help us out with global warming. They could also make it worse.

It appears that the clouds are actually getting worse at protecting us from sunlight. They're moving away from the topics, i.e., the areas where they would be more effective at blocking the sunlight.

> The atmospheric circulation, that large-scale motion of air and water in the atmosphere, is changing, and clouds are going with it. On large scales, clouds seem to be moving from the tropics toward the poles. It's kind of like your grandparents in reverse. And this matters, because if your job is to block incoming sunlight, you are going to be much more effective in the tropics under that intense tropical sun than you are in higher latitudes. So if this keeps up, this will also make global warming worse. And what we have not found, despite years of looking, is any indication of the opposite. There is no observational evidence that clouds will substantially slow down global warming. The earth is not going to break its own fever.

So, if natural clouds can't save us from the climate catastrophe on their own, we could try to engineer our way out of it with [artificial clouds].
