# [Why Clinicians Let Their Computers Make Mistakes](https://medium.com/backchannel/the-self-driving-hospital-fbc5af75ecbe)

> All complex organizations harbor many “latent errors,” unsafe conditions that are, in essence, mistakes waiting to happen.

A catastrophe is never the fault of just one system. James Reason contends that catastrophic failure occurs after several systems fail at once.

> Still, there are legions of errors every day in complex organizations that don’t lead to major accidents. Why? Reason found that these organizations have built-in protections that block glitches from causing.

His Swiss cheese model helps visualize this idea. A laser can only penetrate a slice of Swiss cheese if it hits one of its holes. If another slice covers all the holes in the first, the laser can't penetrate them. This is what a completely protected system looks like.

> Most errors are caused by good, competent people who are trying to do the right thing, and that bolstering the system — shrinking the holes in the Swiss cheese or adding overlapping layers — is generally far more productive than trying to purge the system of human error, an impossibility.

When a nurse administered 38.5 times the antibiotic to a patient, she could've stopped to think critically about whether this dosage was right, but she deferred to the computer instead. Automation complacency silences critical thinking in people.

> She trusted something she believed was even more infallible than any of her colleagues: the hospital’s computerized bar-coding system.

Human critical thinking is an additional slice in Reason's Swiss cheese model. It should not be discounted.

> Humans have a bias toward trusting the computers, often more than they trust other humans, including themselves.

The computerized system has holes in it, and if the human isn't there to fill those, nothing else is there to block the errors sipping through.
