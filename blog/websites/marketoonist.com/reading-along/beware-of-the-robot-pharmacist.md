# [Beware of the Robot Pharmacist](https://medium.com/backchannel/beware-of-the-robot-pharmacist-4015ebf13f6f)

> In tech-driven medicine, alerts are so common that doctors and pharmacists learn to ignore them — at the patient’s risk.

Alerts are to be used sparingly.

> Every alert makes it less likely that people will pay attention to the next one.

The healthcare system does not use alerts sparingly. Healthcare workers see alerts half the time they're fulfilling a medication order.

> Of roughly 350,000 medication orders per month, pharmacists were receiving pop-up alerts on nearly half of them.

That's why healthcare professionals are taught to ignore the alerts.

> Every training program has a “hidden curriculum” (the way things are actually done around here, as opposed to what the policies say or what the administrators told you during that interminable orientation). One of them — passed down from senior residents to the newbies — was, “Ignore all the alerts.”

When asked about whether she experiences interruptions during her job, a nurse answers with a strong affirmative.

> In going through one patient’s medication orders, I’ll be interrupted six or seven times, at least.

Interruptions force people into an unnatural state of multitasking. The higher the cognitive load, the more errors there are.

> Performance degrades rapidly when people try to do several things simultaneously.

This leads to quantifiable reduction of safety in hospitals.

> Every interruption increased the risk of a medication error by 13 percent. When a nurse was interrupted four times, the rate of errors likely to lead to permanent harm or death doubled.

In the Swiss cheese model, catastrophic failure can only find its way into normal operations if it can pass through the holes in every safeguard. However, hospitals can often seem to be made not of safeguards but rather of mines ready to detonate at any moment.

> Social, logistical, and cognitive land mines to sidestep.

When a patient suffers a severe overdose, and it's found that he got administered 38.5 times the dose he was prescribed, that's a catastrophic failure of the healthcare system for this patient. In this case, the hospital blamed the computer software for the mistake.

> It could leave the unit [mg versus mg/kg] box blank, forcing the doctor to make a choice every time, which would actually require that the physician stop and think about it, but few systems do that because of the large number of additional clicks it would generate.

The design of the software has made it easy to stumble into errors like this.

> Computer experts call this type of problem — when the same action can result in two very different results — a “mode error,” and it is especially problematic when the user is not focused on the mode (in this case, mg versus mg/kg).

It is understandable that the root cause of the medication dosing failure here was attributed to software design. Think about how easy it is to miss that one has inadvertently hit a caps lock key.

> The most common mode error in day-to-day computing is activation of the caps lock key, which changes the output of all the other keys.

If you're forcing your users to choose a mode, expect them to make a mistake.

> Computer designers try hard to avoid having any modes at all, but the caps lock key is a major convenience, and so it has survived. When you’re stuck with a mode problem, user-centered design principles dictate that the mode should be made obvious to the user. And so most computer manufacturers incorporate a light into the caps lock key to show when it’s activated, and they signal (with a little up-facing icon) that the caps lock key is on.

The more similar the two modes look in the program, the likelier the mode error is to go unnoticed.

> There is no difference between a minuscule overdose — going 0.1 milligram over a recommended dose — and this very large overdose. They all look exactly the same.
