The length of a customer support call is a bad performance metric.

> A whistleblower who worked in customer service at Navient has said that one major metric employees were graded on wasn't how helpful they could be to customers, but how fast they could get them off the phone.

This is another reason to keep your calls with a customer service rep brief. They may be graded on the time spent with you on the phone.

> Part of our training was keeping your calls to seven minutes. If I don't keep my call to seven minutes, I have a lower status at the end of the day.

If you don't hurry up, the rep may hang up on you "accidentally" to keep quota.

> A few of my coworkers said that there was just no way to answer all of those questions in a way that was satisfactory and maintain the numbers that we were maintaining. And so they would be on their call and then they would just press a button and go, *Oops!*

Bad performance metrics are as widespread across the private sector as bad budget allocations are widespread in the public sector. And yet, instead of targeting the ones that would be easy to argue against, Republicans are picking the hardest ones and then they have to resort to arguing against them in bad faith. For example, most arguments voiced against student debt relief seem to be bad arguments.

> For all the populist speechifying that essentially boils down to *how dare you spend money on something that benefits someone who is not me*, the government spends money all the time on all sorts of things to benefit select individuals because we think there is a net societal benefit. From forgiving loans to small businesses to subsidizing corn farmers, to giving homeowners massive tax breaks, to building stadiums. I don't love spending money on all those things, but if you do, it feels pretty weird to suddenly draw a hard line at student debt.

A good argument would answer how there's not societal benefit to student debt relief, and we're yet to hear such an argument.
