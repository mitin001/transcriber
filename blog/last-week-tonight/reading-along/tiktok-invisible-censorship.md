# [Invisible Censorship](https://theintercept.com/2020/03/16/tiktok-app-moderators-users-discrimination/)

If you are ugly, poor, or have a disability, TikTok will try to bury your content.

> The Chinese video-sharing app with hundreds of millions of users around the world, instructed moderators to suppress posts created by users deemed too ugly, poor, or disabled for the platform.

This is an extension of the Chinese authoritarian regime, specifically the censorship this country's dictator imposes on his subjects. The goal of this level of censorship is to construct a contrived version of China not only in the minds of his subjects but also the outsiders.

> TikTok controls content on its platform to achieve rapid growth in the mold of a Silicon Valley startup while simultaneously discouraging political dissent with the sort of heavy hand regularly seen in its home country of China. On TikTok, livestreamed military movements and natural disasters, video that “defamed civil servants,” and other material that might  threaten "national security" has been suppressed alongside videos showing rural poverty, slums, beer bellies, and crooked smiles. One document goes so far as to instruct moderators to scan uploads for cracked walls and "disreputable decorations" in users' own homes — then to effectively punish these poorer TikTok users by artificially narrowing their audiences.

We know this because The Intercept got ahold of two internal documents from TikTok.

> One lays out bans for ideologically undesirable content in livestreams, and another describes algorithmic punishments for unattractive and impoverished users.

The Intercept is not buying the explanation provided by TikTok spokesperson Josh Gartner, who said that these measures were taken not as an exercise in discrimination but rather to discourage bullying.

> Gartner would not explain why a document purportedly aimed at "preventing bullying" would make zero mention of bullying, nor why it offers an explicit justification of attracting users, not protecting them.

The TikTok algorithm is a black box, but we're starting to uncover some rules that govern it: the traits the Chinese government considers to be understandable are to be filtered out.

> TikTok videos are funneled to a vast audience based on secret criteria. Although what it takes to earn a spot on the “For You” section remains a mystery, the document reveals that it took very little to be excluded, all based on the argument that uploads by unattractive, poor, or otherwise undesirable users.

The document spells out undesirable human traits in both uncomfortably precise and maddeningly vague terms.

> “Abnormal body shape,” “ugly facial looks,” dwarfism, and “obvious beer belly,” “too many wrinkles,” “eye disorders.”

The document also attempts to define undesirable environments.

> Videos in which “the shooting environment is shabby and dilapidated,” including but “not limited to … slums, rural fields” and “dilapidated housing” were also systematically hidden from new users, though “rural beautiful natural scenery could be exempted.”

No one is allowed to show the ugly side of China on TikTok, even those who didn't mean to.

> Special care should be given to check for slummy features such as a “crack on the wall” or “old and disreputable decorations.” The mere appearance of residential disrepair or crooked teeth in the frame, the document shows, could mean the difference between worldwide distribution and relative invisibility..The justification here, as with “ugly” uploaders, was again that TikTok should retain an aspirational air to attract and hold onto new users.

The rules set forth by the documents hint at an admission that much of China's history is ugly.

> Broadcasts deemed to be endangering “national honor and interests” were punished with a permanent ban, as was “uglification of countries’ history,” including Tiananmen Square.

The harshest punishment TikTok can impose on its users is for an offense that is purposely left open to interpretation.

> “Disrupting national unity,” left undefined, comes with a permanent suspension.

The moderators and their supervisors are essentially given free rein over who can be forever banned from the Chinese social media platform. It goes without saying that, in the spirit of national unity, TikTok itself is not to be criticized.

> Users that disparaged TikTok via livestream were to be suspended for three days, and the ones who promote TikTok’s competitors could be banned forever.

Meanwhile, this company employs people whose job is specifically stealing the most popular videos from other platforms. They are tasked with keeping TikTok interesting.

> TikTok has influenced content on its platform not just by censoring videos and disappearing users, but by padding feeds with content from “shadow accounts” operated by company employees posing as regular users.

ByteDancers conducted unauthorized downloads of a massive number of videos from Instagram, and then suddenly #BeachGirl content started showing up on TikTok.

> ByteDance employees scoured Instagram for popular topics, downloaded the videos, and reshared them on TikTok to maintain a steady spray of appealing content; workers tasked with populating “Nice Looking” videos on the app were encouraged to check out Instagram posts tagged with “#BeachGirl,” for example.

And yet, honest users who want to post their own original, not stolen, content of this nature must be very careful not to wander into a territory of insufficient modesty.

> Streams depicting someone wearing bikini or swimming suits outside of a “beach or swimming occasion” were to be punished with a one-week suspension, while accounts showing the “outline of female nipples” could be closed “forever.”

This prudishness is in the spirit of a Silicon Valley startup aching to capture as wide an audience as possible. The rest of the rules are a repressive regime bleeding through the bureaucracy.

> Their ever-shifting decisions about what’s censored and what’s boosted are dictated by Chinese staff, whose policy declarations are then filtered around TikTok’s 12 global offices, translated into rough English, finally settling into a muddle of Beijing authoritarianism crossed with the usual Silicon Valley prudishness.

The rules are purposely left open to subjective interpretation.

> The matter of who decides what ugliness means for hundreds of millions of people in cultures around the world, what “disreputable” decor might mean, or how many wrinkles are considered “too many wrinkles” remain glaringly open and unaddressed, even in the internal moderator documents.

The rules are subject to change at any time with no notice.

> The actual rules remain always on the verge of revision, revocation — or disavowal via corporate statement.
