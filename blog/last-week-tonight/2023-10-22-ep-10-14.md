The list of criticisms that can be levied against McKinsey is extensive.

> McKinsey's advice can be expensive but obvious, its predictions can be deeply flawed, and it's arguably helped supercharge economic inequality in this country.

McKinsey has strong ties to Saudi Arabia.

> It is so deeply entrenched in the government of Saudi Arabia that Saudi Arabia's planning ministry has been dubbed the Ministry of McKinsey.

McKinsey was the intersection of the FDA and Purdue. It was a major source of the conflicts of interest between this regulator and the company it was supposed to be regulating.

> There were multiple instances of McKinsey consultants working for both Purdue and the FDA at the same time. At one point, four consultants working on an FDA contract to enhance drug safety were simultaneously working for Purdue on projects designed to persuade the FDA of the safety of Purdue's products. One project even had them writing 'scripts' for Purdue to use in a meeting with the FDA on the safety of pediatric Oxycontin.

McKinsey benefited greatly from this arrangement.

> It made nearly $84 million in fees from Purdue for advice on how to "turbo-charge" sales of Oxycontin.

McKinsey persuaded AT&T to drop out of the cell phone market right before the cell phone boom. The exorbitant amounts of money AT&T paid McKinsey for this advice were dwarfed by the missed economic opportunity.

> Famously, in the early 1980s, its client AT&T asked McKinsey to estimate how many cell phones would be in use in the world by the turn of the century, and McKinsey concluded that the total market would only be about 900,000 phones total, which persuaded AT&T to pull out of the market for a while. And to be fair, McKinsey were only off by around 739 million, or 82,000%.
