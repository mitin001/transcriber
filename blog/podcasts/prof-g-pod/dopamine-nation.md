Dr. Anna Lembke cautions that addiction can creep up on a person without him realizing it.

> We're not always the best self observers around whether or not our enjoyment that's really leading up to our short term enjoyment is really leading to long-term enjoyment or is interfering, you know, in ways that we can't see just because

Drugs can cloud our judgement. They can make us poor judges of whether consuming the drug constitutes self-destructive behavior.

> These drugs tend to interfere with our insight in terms of what they're doing to us.

People taking drugs lose the ability to tell whether they are addicted.

> Often they can be causing harm or we can be getting addicted and really not see it.

Addiction is difficult to diagnose. Imagine how much more difficult it is to self-diagnose.

> There's no blood test or brain scan to diagnose addiction. We base it on a phenomenology.

We rely on the four Cs of addiction in the DSM (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders).

> Control, compulsions, cravings, and consequences, especially, continued use despite consequences.

There will come a time in an addict's life where whether by a medical professional, people around him, or simply introspection, he will notice that this behavior must stop but he is powerless to stop it.

> Aristotle talked about wide eyed incontinence. _I see the thing that I am doing. I have wide eyes when I'm seeing it. I want to stop doing as much of it as I'm doing and yet I am unable to._

Addiction is an adaptation. The human brain evolved in a world of scarcity, where if one discovered a source of food, sex, etc, he had to obsess over it and not let it go because he or his lineage wouldn't survive without it.

> It's because of the way we are wired over, you know, many, many, many thousands of years of evolution to reflexively approach pleasure and avoid pain, because that is what ensured our survival in a world of scarcity and ever-present danger, which is the world that we lived in for most of human existence.

Since then, the civilization has progressed, and we've discovered and applied technology to ensure addicting things would no longer be scarce.

> Now we've drugified everything. We've made it more potently rewarding, more easily accessible, more abundant, more novel. And so now we're all struggling with this problem of compulsive overconsumption, which is, uh, you know, really making us unhappy.

Addiction makes us unhappy because surges in dopamine force our brain to overcompensate and lower our dopamine baseline thereby putting us in a constant state of melancholy and drug seeking with short-lived moments of euphoria upon drug taking.

> Ultimately what happens with that huge surge of dopamine is that our brain compensates by downregulating dopamine transmission, not just to tonic baseline levels, but actually below baseline. We go into a dopamine deficit state. That is the addicted brain. Now we need more of our drug and more potent forms, not to get high and feel good,
but just to sort of level our balance, go back to baseline and feel normal. And we're in a constant state of craving. Plus, we're experiencing the universal symptoms of withdrawal from any addictive substance, which are anxiety, irritability, insomnia, dysphoria, and craving. Getting more of our drug temporarily relieves that, but it doesn't last very long and actually makes the problem worse.

The lower dopamine baseline and being dopesick are incompatible with maintaining relationships with other people. Addiction becomes more important than social connections.

> Drugs in all their forms are the great human replacement.
Addiction is a disease of loneliness. Even if we have a lot of great people in our lives, if we are, if we get addicted, we will isolate and we will use our drug to replace that human connection.

Quite literally, addiction puts the brain at a diseased state.

> An addiction is a brain disease, a very serious and potentially life threatening one. And until you've either experienced it yourself or seen it in somebody you care
about deeply, it's really hard to imagine how people could get to a place where they would sacrifice everything in, in pursuit of their drug.

The diseased state is characterized by a person, a social animal by its nature, turning into a hermit.

> We're creating a generation of mole people. ... The animals who never go out and never leave their little hidey holes.

It is a myth that addiction is a disease of loneliness. Addiction makes people lonely, not the other way around.

> We sometimes talk about loneliness as the cause of addiction, but more often than not what I see is that the addiction causes the loneliness, that because we're able to use this drug or this device or this behavior to meet
our physical, emotional, sexual needs, we are no longer seeking out other people.

If loneliness doesn't cause addiction, what does? As with all diseases, it all comes down to nature vs. nurture.

> The risks I usually classify into nature, nurture and neighborhood.

A person can be predisposed to addiction on the genetic level, but if they never try the drug or addicting behavior, they'll never develop an addiction.

> We know that trauma contributes to the risk of addiction. That's the nurture part of it, but also neighborhood is really key. And this is again, the ecosystem that we live in. The easier it is to get your drug of choice, the more of it you'll use, the more you'll change your brain and the more likely you will be to develop a very serious addiction.

To reduce the risk of developing an addiction, create friction between yourself and your drug of choice.

> Because we are creatures innately designed to approach pleasure and avoid pain, we need to create spaces where we have access to healthy sources of pleasure and a sufficient challenge to make that interesting enough for us that we creatures who need a certain degree of friction, find it interesting. And also spaces that limit our access to unhealthy sources of pleasure, unhealthy dopamine, as in the instant pleasures of the various intoxicants we've been talking about.

Do not allow addicting foods in your house. Make addicting behaviors hard to engage in. Structure your day so there's never an opportunity to access porn. Stop going to gas stations that tempt you with lottery tickets. Set your alarm clock in such a way that you don't have to interact with the phone first thing in the morning. You can even reincorporate the addicting behaviors into your life, but only if there is enough friction to keep them from becoming harmful. Even alcohol can be consumed responsibly.

> There's a J shape curve that shows that people who drink one to two standard drinks per week are the healthiest, but it's probably because there are, confounds there. Like those are people who do a lot of things in moderation. They eat in moderation, they exercise in moderation. They're even healthier than people who don't drink at all, but that's not because alcohol itself is good for us. It's because in that non-drinking cohort, you get people who are what we call sick quitters who used to drink heavily, and now we're on the liver transplant list.

Do not become the sick quitter. Act on your addictions early, create friction, put enough distance between them and yourself so your dopamine baseline remains at a sufficiently normal level where you can keep forming relationships with people and lead a healthy life.
