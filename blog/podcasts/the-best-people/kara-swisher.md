# [Kara Swisher](https://s3.amazonaws.com/writecomments.com/transcripts/6e04c521168e9a24cd7ca7473a2467b9.csv)

Kara lets us in on her secret for success: she puts her stake in new technology while it's still in its infancy. She is an early adopter. She was among the first to make the transition from print journalism to the Internet. Then, she made the jump into podcasting. Kara got the idea from Steve Jobs and Apple.

> They, of course, started the iPod, right, and created that whole genre of listening and having the intimate relationship with people. And it started with music. But we did an interview, one of the last interviews he did, where he talked about podcasting and he had sort of created the genre in many ways. And he said, it's the iPod and broadcasting: podcasting.

The intimate relationship one has with their phone would extend to the content accessible on it. We would then no longer feel as strong a connection to content found outside the phone. Kara made sure her content would take a prominent position on the phone, and she succeeded. Kara is now among the top podcasters globally. This is no small feat, given how competitive this space has become. The lesson here is that we want to learn from the best, and Apple is certainly the best. Another lesson Kara learned from Apple was the power of abstraction.

> Steve Jobs once said, _The key to Apple was they took complex ideas and made them simple, complex things and made them simple._ And what too many people do is they take simple things and make them complex.

Simplicity brings people joy whereas complexity upsets them. You can never accomplish success by baffling people with nonsense. Google would never have become successful if every time we wanted to look something up on the Internet, we had to fill out a long form with all kinds of adjustable parameters. Instead, Sergey Brin and Larry Page hid all the complexity behind a single search bar. Early on, Kara would visit them and watch what people across the world were putting into the Google search bar.

> There was a scroll that went by of all the words that people were searching at that moment.

She marveled at this simple, yet powerful idea.

> I would sit there just riveted.	Like,	_what are they searching for?_ And then I sort of started to realize this was the expression of humanity. Like you could see into the thought bubble. What are the questions people want to know?

By watching Google searches, we can read each other's thoughts.

> This is the brain of humanity and what it's asking for.

Watching all Google searches happening in real time is like reading the thoughts of humanity as one large organism. Similarly, watching interactions on social media is like watching the collective organism of humanity having an endless conversation with itself. The collective consciousness is real: it is in social media.

> It's humanity talking to itself.

It reflects the schizophrenic, dissonant complexity of the organism of which we are all a part.

> Sometimes it's really vile, which is what humanity is. And sometimes it's absolutely delightful.

Of course, once this conversation has started, it can never stop, as long as humanity is alive. We are all addicted to our phones. We are addicted to googling and posting.

> The only other industry that says the word user is a drug industry.

The collective brain of humanity is rotting away on social media, unable to tear itself from this never-ending conversation. Is this the demise of humanity? Is the collective consciousness so far gone that it is unable to stop driving itself insane? Perhaps, there is no simple idea that could get us out of this mess.
