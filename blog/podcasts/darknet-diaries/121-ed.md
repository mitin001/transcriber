# [121](https://darknetdiaries.com/transcript/121/). Ed

Hacking can be easy. You could watch your victim type in a password, remember it, and then type it yourself later to take over their account. There exists malware that can automate this task. Install a keylogger on their computer and watch what they're typing on their keyboard remotely, without being present in the room with them. Similarly, wireless remotes for cars or garage doors send passwords over the air to their receivers commanding them to unlock or open the doors. They encode such passwords into electromagnetic frequencies and broadcast them across a short distance. If the receiver is within range, it will get the wireless signal, and if the encoded password is correct, it will unlock the door. However, if you have a device nearby that listens to such encoded passwords and records them, you could then replay the same signal, and unlock the car without having access to the wireless car key.

> When you push Unlock, that sends a wireless signal to the car which says unlock the doors.  It’s sometimes possible to use another radio to capture that wireless signal that the remote sent.  So, you’ve eventually captured the unlock code that’s used to unlock the car.  So, now that you have that, you can just replay that packet back, sending it to the car and unlock the car doors whenever you want.  That’s how a replay attack works.

A spoofing device that can replay wireless signals can be used to duplicate any wireless remote as long as it doesn't use timestamping or other methods of authentication.

> A replay attack is when an attacker captures a packet and then is able to send that packet again themselves whenever they wanted.

Penetration tester Ed Skoudis once found that a talking doll he was testing was susceptible to a replay attack. It could be hacked in such a way that it wouldn't stop talking. The more it talked, the hotter it got.

> If you play enough of these messages quickly enough, the doll starts to heat up.

An overheating doll was a potential fire hazard and could burn a child.

> Our initial report said there is a significant chance that someone could scald a child.

Had Ed reported a replay attack vulnerability without such an explanation, the manufacturer wouldn't have fixed it because an overtalking doll is not a significant concern to their consumer. However, because Ed took the time to think through the worst-case consequences of such an attack and included it in the report, the manufacturer had no choice but to fix it.
