# [Tupperware](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/1238535892)

Earl Tupper created tupperware from a byproduct found in abundance in DuPont's chemical plants after WWII.

> When World War II ended, the multinational chemical company, DuPont, reached out and asked if Tupper could figure out what to do with this material they'd developed, a hard, brown slag product they called polyethylene.

There was a lot of polyethylene left over from DuPont's manufacturing processes for helmets, so Tupper turned it into food containers and made a fortune.

> It was like a byproduct of what the military would use for helmets, a product no one else would consider even using.

The product was made popular among housewives by talented saleswomen like Brownie Wise.

> When Brownie Wise saw Tupperware, she immediately knew how to explain it to her customers, how to make it comprehensible and also desirable. She started bringing it into women's homes and demonstrating its effectiveness in ways that would blow their minds.

She understood her audience and knew how to present the product in a way that would sell it to them.

> She would take the Wonder Bowl, she'd fill it up with grape juice, seal it, and then throw it across the room, in somebody's family room. And they'd be aghast, but it wouldn't spill a drip.

She used analogies that were relatable to housewives.

> After a meal, you burp your baby. After a meal, you burp your Tupperware. It is a small act of care toward your leftovers.

Burping tupperware containers was unnecessary, but regulars at tupperware parties eagerly followed this ritual nonetheless.

> You burp a Tupperware just before sealing it completely, by pressing down on the center of the lid while holding up one of the corners, forcing a little burp of air out, and ostensibly locking in freshness.

A savvy, successful businesswoman was not a staple of the American society of the time. Tupperware parties filled this void.

> In the late '40s and early '50s, selling Tupperware, something that happened almost entirely in the female sphere, was a socially-sanctioned way for women to bring in money, to be a part of the working world, but one in which business degrees and special training were less valuable than a wide social circle, an eye for presentation, and the personal experience, charm, and authority to recommend a product.
